Background and Task A large road vehicle picks up lateral vibration from the road
and from its own body modes, and the standard cure — stiffening the suspension or
adding a passive damper — trades ride comfort against payload and can never be tuned
to one frequency without degrading another. This paper studies active suppression with
two dual-motor centrifugal actuators, each driving a pair of counter-rotating eccentric
masses. On the official 2025 data set we answer three questions: how the vehicle re-
sponds with and without the given rotation; how to design a rotation that minimises the
                               RT
lateral vibration index Ih = T1 0 a2y dt; and how to do the same when only the body
acceleration is measurable.
     Modeling Framework The eight eccentric masses are summed analytically, each
contributing 2mr(ω 2 cos θ + α sin θ), which gives an exact actuator force and, with
the four tyre springs and dampers lumped into one equivalent set, a single-degree-of-
freedom oscillator of mass 2 800 kg, stiffness 7 225 200 N/m and damping 3 600 N·s/m:
8.0847 Hz, ζ = 0.0127, so the index is dominated by whatever happens to the line that
falls on the resonance. The key structural fact is that a counter-rotating pair is a single-
line source, Fpair = 4mrω 2 cos κ cos(ωt + ψ), with κ setting amplitude and ψ phase,
both continuously modulated inside |ω| ≤ 100 rad/s, |α| ≤ 5 000 rad/s2 .
     Results per Question Actuator off, scenario 1 gives Ih = 43.702; with the pre-
scribed rotation, 189.293 — the given sequence makes the vibration 4.33 times worse,
and the sign convention reverses the answer to 156.901, so the audit is part of the an-
swer. For scenario 2 a static cancellation of the resonant line reaches 71.053 (−99.46%);
a causal 0.25 s demodulation window that tracks its drift reaches 11.778 (−99.91%) at
38.2 kN of 404 kN, and the window is a genuine interior optimum, not a tuning artefact.
From acceleration alone, a demodulated Newton loop reaches 2 418.9 (−81.6%), and
we show why it cannot reach the open-loop value. The window’s delay, not its gain, is
the real bottleneck, so we train a 1 410-parameter network to forecast the drifting phasor
0.12 s ahead from eight causal features: on unseen sequences it cuts the median phase
error of linear extrapolation from 13.36◦ to 3.39◦ , closed on the official record it gives
9.211 (−99.93%) at 23.9 kN — 37% less force than the analytic design — and driven by
ay alone, after one round of own-state aggregation, 25.423 (−99.81%), 95 times better
than the analytic loop on the same information.
     Validation and Limitation The force was verified to 0.3◦ in phase and the transfer
function to 2.5% in magnitude; doubling and quadrupling the passive damping only
reaches 4 897.3 and 1 447.0. The dominant limitation is phase: a 6◦ command error
raises the index from 11.778 to 128.98, and a 10 ms transport delay lifts the closed loop
to 3 323.5. The learned controller adds two of its own: its corpus is synthetic, built from
three measured statistics of the official record, so it is accurate there but not proof of
generalisation; and it is a model inversion, degrading to 9 964.7 at c − 50%, which is
why the model-based trim is kept.

Keywords: centrifugal actuator; counter-rotating pair; single-degree-of-freedom vehi-
cle model; resonant-line cancellation; causal demodulation; learned phasor forecasting;
own-state aggregation

                                 I. Introduction

1 . 1 Background
     Vibration is the quiet tax that industrial machinery pays for speed. In a road vehicle
the lateral channel is the worst offender, because the body mode that carries it sits low
— around 8 Hz for a loaded heavy chassis — where road roughness, wheel imbalance
and driveline orders all have energy, and because that mode has almost no damping: the
numbers in this problem imply ζ = 0.0127, i.e. a decay time of 1.56 s, which is a bell
rather than a filter.
     Passive remedies are the classical answer and they are honest but blunt [4]. Stiff-
ening the suspension moves the resonance up, which helps at 8 Hz and hurts at 12 Hz;
adding damping helps everywhere a little and costs ride comfort in the 1–2 Hz range
where the driver lives. Worse, a passive element cannot know which line of the spec-
trum is the problem today.
     A centrifugal actuator can. Two eccentric masses on one shaft, spun by a servo
motor, produce a rotating force of magnitude mrω 2 whose direction is the crank angle;
the same shaft reversed gives a second force, and the pair therefore produces a sinusoid
at the rotation frequency whose amplitude and phase are set by the two crank angles.
Because the force grows with ω 2 , a 100 rad/s machine with 100 kg at 0.2 m can generate
tens of kilonewtons from a package that weighs a few hundred kilograms — the reason
this mechanism appears in helicopter hub-moment cancellation [1, 2] and in automotive
active mounts [3].


1 . 2 Restatement of the Problems
     The vehicle carries two such actuators, one at each diagonal, each driving four
eccentric masses in two counter-rotating pairs. The body is connected to the four wheels
by springs and dampers, the wheels are assumed not to move laterally, and the body has
one lateral degree of freedom y(t) measured from its static position. With ay = ÿ the
relative lateral acceleration, the quantity to be minimised is

     Question 2 uses scenario 2, whose disturbance is not the same record, and asks for

     Question 3 keeps scenario 2 but removes the luxury of knowing the disturbance:

1 . 3 Our Work
     Section 2 derives the actuator force and the vehicle equation from first principles
and calibrates both against the data. Section 3 answers Question 1 and shows that the
prescribed sequence is a worsening input, which we explain spectrally rather than dis-
miss. Section 4 turns the counter-rotating pair into a controllable single-line source and
designs two controllers for Question 2: a static cancellation and a causal tracking law,
with the window length identified as an interior optimum and the result cross-checked
against the physical lower bound. Section 5 answers Question 3 with a demodulated
Newton loop driven only by ay , reports its tuning surface and parameter sensitivity, and
documents the five alternative routes we implemented and measured before rejecting
them. Section 6 then attacks the bottleneck the three analytic sections share, which is
the delay of a causal demodulator, by training a small network to forecast the drifting
phasor 0.12 s ahead, with the forecast used both as an open-loop command and as the
starting point of the Newton loop; the training protocol, its held-out accuracy and its
failure modes are reported rather than assumed. Section ?? collects the error analysis,
including the two effects that dominate any real installation: command phase error and
transport delay.
     Four things in this paper are, as far as we can tell, not in the other solutions to this
problem. (i) The pair is treated as an exact single-line source with independent amplitude
and phase channels (eq:pair), which turns the design problem from a search over angle
sequences into a two-parameter modulation problem with no inequality constraints to
police. (ii) The window length is shown to be an interior optimum (an 18-point sweep)
whose position is predicted by the demodulator’s group delay, not a hyper-parameter
to be tuned. (iii) The prescribed sequence is reported as harmful and the harm is de-
composed spectrally, with a sign and reference-axis audit that reproduces the published
numbers under the opposite convention. (iv) A learned forecaster replaces the average,
is measured on sequences it never saw, and its own failure at half damping is reported
together with the trim that bounds it.

                               II. Model Building

2 . 1 Assumptions
  1. One lateral degree of freedom. Yaw, roll and the wheels’ own lateral motion are
     neglected, as the problem instructs. Justification: the four suspension points move
     together in the lateral direction at the frequencies of interest, and the body is stiff
     compared with the suspension. Used throughout; the consequence is that the model
     cannot represent the torsional mode near 2 Hz, which the data do not excite either.
  2. Linear suspension, lumped parameters. The four corner springs and dampers
     are replaced by one equivalent stiffness k and damping c acting on the relative
     displacement y. Justification: the data set supplies single values for both, so the
     problem already assumes this reduction.
  3. Rigid crank, ideal servo. The connecting rods and motor inertias are massless
     and the commanded crank angle is realised exactly, except where we deliberately
     add a delay in Section ??. Justification: the problem states the simplification, and
     it keeps the force law exact rather than introducing an unidentifiable lag.
  4. The two actuators act in parallel on the same coordinate. Justification: both
     are bolted to the same rigid body, so their force vectors add.
  5. Disturbance is exogenous. The recorded lateral force is imposed and unaffected
     by the body motion. Justification: it represents road input through the tyres, which
     a 2 800 kg body cannot push back.

2 . 5 Numerical scheme and its verification
     Equation (4) is integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at a 1 ms step, with the
two forces linearly interpolated inside each 10 ms sample interval; the index (1) is
the trapezoidal mean of a2y . Derivatives of a sampled crank angle are taken by cen-
tral differences followed by a projection of ∆ω onto a symmetric band of half-width
αmax ∆t, which is the exact discrete constraint the servo imposes; we checked that for
the prescribed sequence this projection never binds, so the data are themselves admis-
sible (|ω|max = 39.07 rad/s, |α|max = 2 462 rad/s2 ).
     Two independent checks were run before any of the results below were trusted.
First, the free-decay envelope of the simulated response was compared with exp(−ζωn t)
and the frequency with ωd = 50.7939 rad/s: both agree to the third digit, with the
envelope rate 0.6413 /s against the analytic 0.6429 /s. Second, the transfer function
from a unit disturbance to the body acceleration was measured by driving the model
with a 1 kN sinusoid at 8 Hz for 20 s and reading the last 10 s: the model gives 10.47
m2 /s per kN at 131.99◦ against the analytic 10.73 at 129.78◦ , i.e. 2.5% in magnitude and
2.2◦ in phase. The residual is the ringing of the initial transient inside the measurement
window, and it is the reason the analytic value, not the measured one, is used inside the
controllers of Sections 4 and 5.

4 . 1 What makes this problem hard

4 . 4 What the design physically is
              Design D is a nonlinear damper, and it is instructive to say so in energy terms.
The mechanical power injected by the actuator is ⟨Fact ẏ⟩ = −115.0 W: it is negative,
so the actuator removes energy from the body, on average, continuously. It does so by
synthesising a force proportional and opposite to the velocity of the 8 Hz component
while leaving the rest of the spectrum alone. That is the precise sense in which it beats a
passive damper: a passive element has one c for all frequencies, whereas this one has an
effective c only at 8 Hz. The measurement supports the claim — doubling the physical
damping (c = 2c0 ) only brings the index to 4 897.25, and quadrupling it to 1 446.99,
still two orders of magnitude away from 11.778, because the resonant line survives a
damper and does not survive a canceller.


4 . 5 Why the static design cannot be saved by more authority
              It is tempting to conclude that the residual of design A is a matter of strength. It
is not. Increasing the depth drive cannot help, because the leftover is not at 8 Hz: it
is the 3 and 5 Hz lines, which the pair could only touch by leaving ω8 , and leaving ω8
means giving up the cancellation of the line that dominates. Figure 4(b) makes the point
literally: the force spectrum of design D contains the 8 Hz line at 38 kN and essentially

4 . 6 Designs we implemented, measured and rejected
              Three further mechanisms were built and evaluated before design D was adopted;
reporting them is part of the answer, because each looks attractive on paper.
Team # SHUWEICUP2503517                                                      Page 11 of 27


     The α channel. Parking the eccentrics at θ = π/2 nulls the ω 2 term and turns
Eq. (2) into F = 2mrα sin θ, i.e. a force proportional to angular acceleration with gain
8mr = 160 N·s2 and a limit of 800 kN. Because α is the second derivative of the crank
angle, a demanded force requires integrating it twice, and the required crank excursion
is ε = F /(8mrw2 ): at 8 Hz that is 0.04 rad, but at 0.5 Hz it is 0.6 rad, where the
lever sin θ collapses. With a Gaussian pre-filter of σ = 0.06 s the design reaches Ih =
253.08 (98.08%) and with σ ≥ 0.10 the fixed-point iteration for the inverse dynamics
diverges (the peak α reaches 1054 ). The best value found in the sweep is still an order
of magnitude worse than design A. Rejected: elegant, wideband, and impossible below
a few hertz.
     A damping line. Using the second pair at the ring frequency to synthesise F =
−ca ẏ looks like a one-line fix. One-shot values are respectable (ca /c = 1 gives 48.73),
but the fixed-point iteration that would make it self-consistent oscillates between 48.9,
76.6 and 48.2, and diverges for ca /c ≥ 1.5, reaching 1 820. Rejected: a single line
cannot follow a residual that drifts.
     Free-form optimisation. Parameterising θg as a 24-term Fourier series in 8 Hz
harmonics and running SLSQP with the speed and acceleration limits as constraints
converges, but the optimum it finds is a slower, more expensive way of arriving at the
same law as Eq. (7) — the harmonics beyond the fundamental buy nothing, because the
only line that matters is the fundamental. Rejected as equal result at higher complexity.
     One pair per line. A reader may ask the obvious question: the machine has two
pairs and the disturbance two lines that matter, so why not give each pair one line?
Measured, it does worse than design D: with the 8 Hz pair tracked and the 5 Hz pair
static, Ih = 12.517 (99.90%), and with both lines tracked, Ih = 2 718.336 (79.33%).
The reason is the modulation index. A pair whose whole capacity is assigned to one line
needs κ = 85.21◦ to produce 16.79 kN, where cos κ = 0.084 is steep: a 0.2% amplitude
error moves the emitted line by 4%, and the 0.25 s demodulation leaks 7.3 kN into 5
Hz. Splitting the capacity of both pairs on the same line lowers κ to 72◦ , where cos κ
is three times less sensitive. The second pair is therefore worth more as capacity on
the dominant line than as authority on a minor one, and two slow trackers fighting each
other (the both-tracked case) is worse still.
     The eccentric mass as part of the body. Because Assumption 4 admits the 8m =
800 kg of eccentric mass into M , the ring sits at 8.08 Hz and scenario 2’s 8.0 Hz line
drives it 1% off resonance. Dropping the eccentric mass, as most published solutions do,
moves M to 2 000 kg, the ring to 9.57 Hz, and the uncontrolled index from 13 148.921
to 209.650. We report both because the difference is a modelling convention, not an
arithmetic one; Sec. 6 . 11 quantifies what it does to the designs.

    V. Question 3: suppression from acceleration alone

5 . 1 What the sensor actually gives
     Question 3 removes the disturbance record. What remains is the body acceleration
ay (t), sampled at 10 ms, in real time; y and v are not measured and the disturbance is
not observable. It is important to be precise about what has and has not been lost. The
crank angles, their derivatives, the actuator force and the vehicle parameters are still
known — the actuator has encoders and the data sheet gives M , k, c. What is unknown
is the phase of the disturbance, which design D read from the disturbance record.
     The natural first idea is to reconstruct the disturbance by inverting the equation of
motion, F̂d = M ay −cv̂−k ŷ with ŷ, v̂ obtained by integrating ay twice. We implemented
it, and it fails: the double integration drifts, the estimate is biased at the resonance by
exactly the term that dominates, and the bias is then fed back through the command,
closing a loop around the error. The measured result is Ih ≈ 1.39 × 104 , i.e. within
15% of doing nothing, with 99.8 kN of actuator force being spent to achieve it. It is also
fragile — a 10% stiffness error moves it to 2 416 and a −50% damping error to 26 469.
Rejected, and reported because it is the obvious route.

5 . 4 Why it cannot reach the open-loop number
              The honest reading of −81.6% against −99.91% is that Question 3 is a different,
harder problem, and the gap has a mechanism rather than a tuning excuse. The loop
nulls the 8 Hz line of ay . But ay ’s 8 Hz content is not only the disturbance-forced part:
the body’s own ring at 8.08 Hz is driven by the 3 and 5 Hz residual, and the loop cannot
distinguish the two inside a two-period window because they are 1% apart in frequency
and the ring decays over 1.56 s. So the loop cancels the ring, the residual immediately re-
excites it, and the estimator sees the regrowth on the next window. The loop converges
to the fixed point where the cancellation of the ring equals its regeneration — which
is a small number, not zero. Design D does not suffer this because it demodulates the
disturbance, where the two contributions are cleanly separated.
              Two consequences follow, both visible in the measurements. First, the achievable
Team # SHUWEICUP2503517                                                      Page 15 of 27


index is bounded away from zero by the ring’s own regeneration, and the bound is no
better than the 71.053 that a static cancellation of the same line achieves in open loop.
Second, the loop leans on the plant model, Eq. (9), to convert an acceleration into a
force, so a parameter error that is harmless in design D becomes an accuracy limit here:

          case k + 10% k − 10% c + 50% c − 50% M + 10% M − 10%

The spread from 457 to 13 601 is a factor of 30 at ±10%, and the favourable cells are
favourable for a structural reason: a 10% stiffer suspension or a 10% lighter body moves
ωn away from the 8 Hz line, which reduces the ring that the loop is fighting. The un-
favourable cells are the ones that make the ring stronger. The loop never becomes un-

 VI. Learning the drift: a trained forecaster in the loop
     Designs A and D are analytic, and they hit a wall that is not about algebra: a causal
demodulator cannot report the present. Its moving average of 25 samples has a group
delay of (25 − 1) × 0.01/2 = 0.12 s, which is 0.96 cycle at 8 Hz, so the phasor it
hands to the controller is the phasor of the recent past. Section ?? then shows why that
is expensive: a 6◦ error in the commanded phase costs one order of magnitude (128.98
against 11.778). Neither can the delay be averaged away, because the line itself is not
stationary — over the 10 s record its amplitude moves between 13.4 and 20.3 kN and
its phase peak-to-peak is 33.4◦ — and the measured window sweep is U-shaped: 0.20 s
overruns the actuator (|ω| = 69 rad/s, Ih = 16.79), 0.40 s lags the drift (53.00), and only
0.25 s sits between them. This section replaces the average by a forecast, and trains it.


6 . 1 What is learnt, and how
     The quantity to predict is the disturbance phasor at t+H with H = 0.12 s, the delay
that has to be undone. The features are eight numbers that a real controller can compute
at time t: the in-phase and quadrature components of the line obtained from the causal
Team # SHUWEICUP2503517                                                         Page 16 of 27


window at four lags (0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20 s). The label is the same two components, taken
from a centred two-cycle window at t + H. The asymmetry is deliberate and is stated
here because it is the kind of shortcut that silently turns a forecast into a cheat: the label
may look at the future, the features never do, and the deployed model is only ever fed
the eight causal numbers.
     The regressor is a two-hidden-layer perceptron, 8-32-32-2 with tanh, i.e. 1 410
trainable parameters, trained with Adam (learning rate 2 × 10−3 , cosine decay, mini-
batches of 2 048, 60 epochs, best held-out epoch 59) on standardised inputs and targets.
Standardising the targets is not cosmetic: with raw newton targets the first-epoch MSE
is 4.5×107 and 400 full-batch steps never leave that plateau (held-out phase error 19.8◦ ),
whereas the standardised mini-batch run converges to a held-out MSE of 0.202 in stan-
dardised units, about 3.4◦ of phase.
     Training data is the other half of the design, because the official workbook contains
exactly one realisation per scenario — one draw of the drift, which is not a training
set. We therefore built a synthetic corpus whose statistics are the measured ones: three
lines at 3.111, 7.760 and 16.788 kN, each with its own random-walk phase whose peak-
to-peak matches the measured 33.4◦ , plus colour noise carrying the measured 6.86%
rms projection residual. 208 sequences of 1 000 samples are split by sequence into 160
training, 24 validation and 24 held-out test sequences, so no window of a test sequence
is ever seen. The real scenario 2 is in none of the three: it is kept as the deployment test,
and it is the number we report.


6 . 2 What the training buys
     On the held-out sequences the median absolute phase error is 3.39◦ and the 95th
percentile 12.52◦ . The obvious physics baseline — take the unwrapped phase of the
causal phasor and extrapolate it linearly at its own local slope — gives 13.36◦ median,
i.e. the learner is 3.9 times more accurate, and it is more accurate precisely where the
extrapolation fails, which is when the drift changes sign.
     Closed on the real scenario 2, with the forecast inserted into the same pair law as
design D, the index is Ih = 9.211, −99.93% against 13 148.921, with |Fact |max = 23.90
kN (5.9% of the 404 kN the two actuators can emit), |ω|max = 65.85 rad/s and |α|max =
2 129.9 rad/s2 . Against design D’s 11.778 at 38.19 kN this is both a lower index and
37% less force: the difference is not authority, it is timing.
     The lead itself is a measurement of the mechanism, not a hyper-parameter. Sweep-
ing it gives 18.172 at 0 ms, 21 500.9 at 60 ms, 10.008 at 90 ms, 9.211 at 120 ms, 7.561
at 150 ms and 15.281 at 180 ms. The spike at 60 ms is the point of the figure: 0.06 s is
Team # SHUWEICUP2503517                                                                                                      Page 17 of 27

6 . 3 From the acceleration alone
     Question 3 forbids measuring the disturbance, so the same forecast is retrained
with the eight features taken from ay instead of from Fd : 48 simulated sequences for
training, 12 for validation, the same architecture and schedule. Deployed as an open-
loop command it does surprisingly well — Ih = 25.423, i.e. −99.81%, with |Fact |max =

(2 418.9).
     It does not get there by itself. The first attempt re-estimated the phasor from
the controlled ay and re-applied it, iterating to a fixed point; the iteration diverges,
3 694.2 → 13 037.8 → 12 882.4, for a reason worth reporting: the network was trained
on uncontrolled acceleration, so the ring that its own force puts into ay is out of distri-
bution, and each pass adds force to a signal the model misreads. The standard remedy
is to let the policy visit its own states and label them: one round of aggregation (run the
loop, record ay , attach the true disturbance phasor as the label, retrain) brings the index
to 25.423. More rounds make it worse, 64.393, 82.822, 161.872. That is not noise: once
the loop cancels part of the drive, two different disturbances produce nearly the same
ay , the aggregated set teaches the learner the average of two answers, and the average
is wrong for both. We deploy round one and say so.


6 . 4 What the learner is not good at

through the nominal plant, so it inherits the plant’s errors. Measured on the same seven
perturbations used for the Newton loop: nominal 25.423, k + 10% 1 527.8, k − 10%

c − 50% cell is the honest failure: with half the real damping the force is sized for a
plant that is not there, and the open-loop command is worse than doing nothing. Sensor
noise, by contrast, barely matters (25.44, 25.69 and 27.00 at 0.5, 2 and 5 m/s2 , against
an acceleration rms of 127.5 m/s2 ).

command. Starting the Newton loop from the forecast gives 108.97 at nominal — worse
than the pure forecast, because feedback on a good command only adds noise — but
it collapses the worst case from 9 964.7 to 1 345.2 and leaves every other cell below

6 . 7 Transport delay
             A delay is a phase error that grows with frequency, and the acceleration path of
Question 3 has two of them (sensor and command). Shifting the realised crank sequence
by one to three samples moves design D from 11.778 to 3 323.5 (10 ms), 12 296.5 (20
ms) and 24 704.8 (30 ms) — the last being nearly twice as bad as no actuator at all,
because a delayed cancellation adds. This sets a hard engineering requirement: the loop
must close inside about 5 ms, i.e. inside a single sample, which on a vehicle means
running the demodulator on the servo drive rather than on a body controller.


6 . 8 Sampling and numerical error
             The record is sampled at 100 Hz, so the 8 Hz line has 12.5 samples per period and
the projection of a non-integer number of periods leaks: the two-period window is cho-
sen precisely because 25 samples span exactly two periods, which makes the leakage
vanish to machine precision. Halving the step (Runge–Kutta at 1 ms instead of 2 ms)
changes the index by less than 0.02%; the trapezoidal index over 1 000 samples is accu-
rate to the same order. The discretisation is therefore not a contributor at the reported
precision.

6 . 9 Accelerometer noise
     Adding white noise to ay at 0.5, 2, 5 and 10 m/s2 (i.e. 0.4% to 7.8% of the un-
controlled root-mean-square acceleration) moves the closed-loop index from 2 418.9 to
2 420.1, 2 426.4, 2 452.0 and 2 533.3. The loop averages the sensor over 25 samples and
only acts once per window, so it is a low-pass filter on its own input; this is the one
sensitivity that is comfortably benign.


6 . 10 Model error
     Beyond the parameter table of Section 5, two structural assumptions carry risk.
The single-degree-of-freedom reduction cannot represent a torsional mode; if the real
body has one between 6 and 10 Hz, the actuator would pump it. The assumption of
an ideal servo is the one we would challenge first in a test rig, because the measured
consequence of a 10 ms delay is larger than the consequence of a 50% damping error.


6 . 11 The eccentric mass convention
     The single largest sensitivity is not a parameter but a definition: whether the 8m =
800 kg of rotating eccentric mass is counted in the body that the actuator pushes. Count-
ing it (Assumption 4) gives the numbers of this paper; dropping it reproduces the Ih ≈
210–226 baseline that most published solutions report for scenario 2, and would put this
paper’s baseline in their range. Table 4 replays the same actuator commands under both
conventions. Nothing in the actuator changes — its force follows from the disturbance
and does not contain M — so the table separates the physics from the convention.

6 . 12 The reference axis, and why our Question 1 conclusion is not

a small change: the prescribed table of scenario 1 then cancels part of the disturbance

                                                 VII. Conclusion
                  This paper models a large vehicle with two dual-motor centrifugal actuators and
answers the three competition questions with numbers that can be recomputed from the
supplied data.
                  For Question 1, the vehicle without the actuator has Ih = 43.702; with the pre-
scribed rotation it has Ih = 189.293, i.e. the prescribed sequence is an aggravating input,
and the sign convention of the pair moves that number between 156.901 and 342.712.
We state the audit rather than picking a convention.
                  For Question 2, the counter-rotating pair is a single-line source whose amplitude
and phase are κ and ψ, and both are free within the actuator limits. A static cancellation
of the resonant line gives Ih = 71.053 at 4.2% of the available force; tracking the line’s
drift with a causal 0.25 s demodulation window gives Ih = 11.778, −99.91% against
the uncontrolled 13 148.921, using 38.19 kN of 404 kN and 58.2 rad/s of 100 rad/s. The
window length is an interior optimum explained by the estimator’s half-window lag,
and the design is physically a damper that acts only at 8 Hz: its mean mechanical power
is −115.0 W, while quadrupling the passive damping reaches only 1 446.99.
                  For Question 3, a demodulated Newton loop on the acceleration phasor reaches
Ih = 2 418.9 (−81.6%) using nothing but ay , and we show that the residual gap to the
open-loop design is caused by the inseparability of the body ring from the drive inside
a two-period window: the loop cancels the ring and the 3/5 Hz residual immediately
re-excites it. The loop is stable over a two-dimensional tuning surface and degrades
gracefully with noise but poorly with parameter error (457 to 13 601 at ±10%).
                  The last section trains the part that the analytic designs could not supply, which is a
Team # SHUWEICUP2503517                                                      Page 24 of 27


view of the future. A 1 410-parameter network, fed by eight causal features and trained
on a synthetic corpus whose three statistics are measured from the official record, fore-
casts the drifting phasor 0.12 s ahead and cuts the median phase error of linear extrap-
olation from 13.36◦ to 3.39◦ on sequences it never saw. Used as the command it gives
Ih = 9.211 (−99.93%) at 23.9 kN, a lower index than the best analytic design with 37%
less force; used as the starting point of the Newton loop it bounds the worst parameter-
error cell from 9 964.7 to 1 345.2. Driven by ay alone the same learner reaches 25.423
(−99.81%), which is 95 times the index of the analytic loop on the same information
and to our knowledge better than the published acceleration-only solutions, at the cost
of being a model inversion: at half the true damping it is worse than doing nothing,
which is why we keep the model-based trim.
     The engineering conclusion is quantitative: the mechanism has 24 times the author-
ity it needs, so the binding constraints are timing and phase, not force. A 6◦ command
error costs an order of magnitude, and a 10 ms delay in the command path lifts the open-
loop design from 11.778 to 3 323.5. The learned forecaster is best understood as buying
timing out of measurement: it costs 69 s of training and eight multiplies per sample, and
it is what closes the gap that no amount of gain tuning could close.


                   VIII. Strengths and Weaknesses

8 . 1 Strengths
  • The actuator force is derived, not fitted: Eq. (3) is exact for counter-rotating pairs
     and was verified to 0.3◦ , so every reported force is traceable to the geometry.
  • The design is closed-form and constraint-respecting by construction (cos κ ∈ [0, 1],
     ω fixed), which is why it needs no projection and no penalty terms and why the
     operating point (58.2 rad/s, 936.8 rad/s2 ) is guaranteed admissible.
  • The claimed improvement is cross-checked three ways: against the physical lower
     bound of cancelling one line (71.053), against passive damping (1 446.99 at 4c0 ),
     and against the feasibility of the command itself (38.19 kN of 404 kN).
  • Every negative result is reported with its measurement, including the prescribed
     angles being harmful, five rejected controller designs and the four DAgger rounds
     that regressed, which makes the positive results falsifiable rather than merely plau-
     sible.
  • The learned component is small enough to audit and is audited: 1 410 parameters,
     eight causal features, one fixed seed, 69 s of training, and its accuracy is quoted on
     held-out sequences rather than on the record it was tuned against.
Team # SHUWEICUP2503517                                                       Page 25 of 27



8 . 2 Weaknesses
  • Phase accuracy is a hard requirement and the paper cannot deliver it: at 6◦ the index
    is 128.98, and a 30 ms delay makes the design worse than doing nothing (24 704.8
    against 13 148.921).
  • Question 3 is answered 2 418.9 by the analytic loop and 25.423 by the learned one,
    both against 11.778 for the open-loop design, so even the trained controller leaves
    a factor of 2.2 on the table when the disturbance is invisible: we close most of the
    gap with a forecast, not all of it.
  • The learned forecaster is trained on a synthetic corpus. Its three statistics (the three
    measured line amplitudes, the measured 33.4◦ phase drift, the measured 6.86%
    residual) come from the official record and the real record is only used as a test,
    which is the honest way to do it and still not the same as training on field data. A
    vehicle whose drift statistics differ from this one would need the corpus rebuilt.
  • The learned command is a model inversion and inherits the plant’s errors: at half
    the true damping it raises the index to 9 964.7, i.e. worse than the actuator off. The
    trim brings that to 1 345.2, but the pair of numbers is a warning, not a guarantee.
  • The single-degree-of-freedom reduction assumes the four corners move together;
    a torsional mode between 6 and 10 Hz would be pumped rather than suppressed,
    and the supplied data cannot rule that out.
  • The disturbance is treated as exogenous. At 16.8 kN against a 2 800 kg body the
    back-reaction is small, but it is not exactly zero, and we have not quantified it.
  • Every headline number depends on a convention we chose and stated: counting
    the 800 kg of eccentric mass gives Ih = 13 148.921 and design D removes 99.91%
    of it; dropping it, as most published solutions do, gives 209.650 and 79.61%, and
    the ranking of the designs inverts (Table 4). We consider the counted version the
    correct one, but a reader comparing papers must renormalise before comparing.
