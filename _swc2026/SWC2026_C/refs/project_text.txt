             Punctuality Is Bought by Rules, Not by Data:
     State Estimation and Scheduling for Restocking Robots
                                          Summary
      This paper asks what actually buys punctuality in the restocking of unmanned supermarkets.
Following one observation record through three questions (what state can be recovered from the
ego-centric recordings, how far it can be predicted once observations stop, and what another
look is worth), we show that almost all of the attainable punctuality is bought by the scheduling
rule, while better state, better prediction, more observation and a larger reservation each fail their
own test. The package holds 69 recordings from 24 environments, 66070 timestamped frames
and 5.834 GB, split into 16/4/4 environments. The three formats differ in frame rate, coordinate
definition and annotation coverage, so we fix one convention and document what it costs.
      For the state question we define a two-layer hand state, continuous (position, orientation,
velocity, aperture, stationary flag) over 5 rule-derived stages, with reliability fused into a soft
weight from a calibrated logistic route and a label-free Mahalanobis route. Held-out records
give AUC 0.596 and 0.627, average precision 0.0407 against a prevalence baseline of 0.0189.
Stage agreement rises from 0.414 (ARI) for chain-constrained agglomeration to 0.5366 (refits
±0.0124) for a learned boundary detector whose decoding constants were selected on validation
records only. We report agreement, not accuracy.
      For the prediction question we mask the dominant-wrist trajectory for 0.2, 0.5, 1.0 and
2.0 seconds and replay four predictors on identical windows. A ridge predictor gives a three-
dimensional RMSE of 2.00 cm at 0.2 s against 2.60 cm for hold-last, and 8.58 against 8.95 cm at
2.0 s, so the advantage decays with the horizon. Conformal radii grow from 0.0289 m to 0.1367
m. Pricing a look at the frozen cost model puts the optimum at 0.30 s with objective 4.521,
below the fixed-interval baselines.
      For the scheduling question we treat service time as affine in the unknown source duration
and derive the punctuality event in closed form: a job finishes on time if and only if its duration
lies below (due − 𝑠 − 𝛽)/𝛼, so the on-time probability is one evaluation of the duration law and
the reservation cancels out of it. Across 8000 executed jobs the Brier is 0.0365 (skill 0.644)
and the realised rate is tracked within 0.0397. On held-out seeds the capacity-aware rule lifts the
weighted on-time rate by 25.28 points in S2 and 21.88 in S3 over EDF; adaptive refinement adds
5.14 and 6.56 points on top. The true duration is worth only 3.33 and 3.85, a paid look is worth
nothing detectable, and an extra reservation moves the rate by -9.83 and -7.99. Punctuality is
bought by the rule, not by the data.
Key word: ego-centric hand state. Observation reliability. Conformal prediction. Value of
information. Stochastic scheduling; reservation idle

1. Introduction
1.1 Task background
      An unmanned supermarket restocks itself through a person: walk to a shelf, pick a product,
carry it, place it, move on. The organisers released ego-centric recordings of that work so that a
model can estimate the hand state, forecast it when the camera loses the hand, and decide when
a restocking job should be scheduled and how much observation it deserves. The questions are
not independent: Part I fixes what Part II extrapolates, and that error is the only honest input to
the review decision of Part III.

1.2 Problem analysis: three questions and one chain
     Part I asks for a state, a reliability notion, a reproducible baseline and failure cases, noting
that a qualified record is a reference, not ground truth. Part II asks how error grows once
observation stops and when a new look is worth its cost, with windows, budgets, per-request
costs and return delays fixed in advance. Part III asks for a policy under an unknown service
time with a reserved buffer, a paid review budget and a capacity limit, on the official seeds.
Table 1 states what each part concludes before any of it is derived, and Part III consumes the
price and the error scale but not the trajectory. Figure 1 draws the object once: a state, a forecast,
and a decision that consumes both.

2. Data and Problem Setting
2.1 Three recording formats and what they share
      The package mixes three formats, and Appendix N gives their scale. EgoDemo ships JSON,
Parquet and MP4 with both wrist views on a subset of recordings. EgoPro ships per-hand CSV
tables plus MP4 with head stereo and both wrist views, and EgoStandard ships the same tables
with head stereo only. Each hand carries 21 annotated three-dimensional keypoints on the stan-
dard twenty-one-point hand layout, and the wrist-camera world extrinsics are absent, so the wrist
views are not commonly calibrated. We work in the head frame and never compare absolute po-
sitions across datasets.
      The mean wrist speed differs across formats, 0.1448, 0.1695 and 0.1673 m/s, which is
consistent with different task mixes, not with different sensors. The dominant hand is the left
one in 26 recordings and the right one in 43, so a single-hand convention loses the other hand
on a minority of records. We keep the dominant hand and record the choice.

2.2 Retention and the oﬀicial split
     The official split assigns 16 environments to training, 4 to validation and 4 to test, and Part
II uses 55 training and 7 test recordings inside that split. A record is one continuous capture
of one environment by one wearer. A time interval is the half-open span [𝑡0 , 𝑡 1 ) between two
timestamps of one record that the split and the segmenter both treat as a unit, and every interval
in this paper belongs to exactly one record. Retention is reported as a funnel in Section 3.5:
frames enter the panel only when both hands are annotated and the frame is not flagged, and
flagged frames are retained as labels, not dropped, because a reliability model without negatives
is not identifiable.

2.3 Documented gaps in the released package
      The statement refers to 5 documentation files (record_index.csv, record_split.csv,
missing_observation_experiments.md, attachment_guide.md and file_manifest.
csv), none of which is present in the package we received. The only protocol document
shipped is the Part III requirements file. Two consequences are recorded here, not hidden:
the environment split is taken from the folder names in the released archives, and the Part II
masking protocol, cost function and review delay are ours, frozen before any experiment and
reported as such wherever they appear. The split separates task classes, not only environments,
and that separation can be priced: retraining per held-out class gives AUC 0.8599 against
0.9079, a gap of 0.0480. Only 3 of the 26 classes score at all. Each also leaves its environment
unseen, so 0.559 bounds the task share from above at 𝑛 = 3 (Appendix E).
      Four further facts would not be guessed from the statement. The archives arrive as flattened
CSV tables whose header carries a UTF-8 byte-order mark, so a reader that assumes plain UTF-
8 silently corrupts the leading column name and every join after it. The manifest declares a head
depth camera at 5 fps and 320×240, but no depth channel is present. No wrist-camera extrinsic
Team CSWBMCM2634892                                                                    Page 6 of 25


is shipped and 24 of the 24 standard recordings have no wrist camera, so the four video views
are not calibrated and are not fused. They are 92.4% of the volume, and we read them only as a
reliability cue (Appendix L), never as policy state. Three of the 349 intervals sit exactly on the
duration threshold, so 343 clear it only under a non-strict reading, which we take.

2.4 What the protocol fixes and what it leaves open
      Three items are fixed by the statement and are not modelling choices. The service time is a
deterministic affine map of the source annotation, 𝑝 = 𝛼𝑑 + 𝛽 with 𝛼 and 𝛽 drawn from the five
published mappings, so an overrun is a property of the sampled source interval and not of our
estimator. The information rule forbids looking at the future of the source interval: at decision
time only the elapsed prefix of the current job is visible, and any information bought with a
review becomes usable only after its delay has elapsed. Source sampling is uniform within a
job’s family, with replacement, driven by the official seeds. The seeds are inputs to the sampler
and never to the policy.
      What the protocol leaves open is metric aggregation (a vector, not a scalar), the tie-break
between rate and cost, and jobs that cannot finish inside the horizon. We prefer weighted on-time
rate to cost, keep every job in the denominators, and charge the horizon as the completion time.

2.5 Assumptions
    Eight assumptions carry the model; Appendix J registers each against the evidence that
supports it.
 1. Source annotations are the reference for hand state. No product-state label is released, so a
    completed grasp is evidence about the hand, never about the shelf.
 2. The keypoint skeleton is a usable proxy for hand configuration; residual calibration error
    enters through the reliability weight rather than being ignored.
 3. The three recording formats can be mapped into one head-centred panel under the docu-
    mented coordinate convention; format-specific residual error is treated as a dataset effect
    and reported, not pooled away.
 4. The service time of a job is affine in its source duration, with the published mapping coef-
    ficients; the coefficients are inputs, not quantities we estimate.
 5. Review delay and review cost are fixed by the protocol and do not depend on system load.
 6. Job durations are independent across jobs within a case, and the official sampler with re-
    placement is the correct description of the arrival process.
 7. A stage is a property of the hand trajectory alone; object and task context can shift its timing
    but not its definition.
 8. Conclusions are about the ordering of policies and mechanisms, not about the absolute
    on-time rate of any particular warehouse.

2.6 One notation for state, observation and decision
      Let 𝑧𝑡 = (𝑥𝑡 , 𝜑𝑡 , 𝑑) be the state of a work unit after 𝑡 seconds of its own service: the
hand configuration 𝑥𝑡 , the phase 𝜑𝑡 among the 5 stages, and the total duration 𝑑, never ob-
served before the unit finishes. The three questions are one belief-state Markov decision process
M = (B, A, 𝑃, 𝑟) with belief 𝛽𝑡 = 𝑃(𝑑, 𝜑𝑡 | 𝑥1:𝑡 , 𝑜 1:𝑡 ), action 𝑎 𝑡 = ( 𝑗, request?) ∈ A, transition
𝑃 driven only by the duration law 𝐹𝑑 and the release sequence, and reward 𝑟 paying the weighted
on-time indicator and charging the protocol cost. The history 𝑜1:𝑡 holds the hands, the per-frame
quality flags and the boundary probabilities, truncated to the released prefix; 𝑢 𝑡 with an optional
review extends that prefix at a cost and a delay; 𝜋 maps 𝛽𝑡 to 𝑢 𝑡 ; 𝐹𝑑 is fitted on training segments
only; 𝑏 is the reservation the policy holds.
      Read this way, the three questions are three projections of one object. The phase is identifi-
able from geometry only when the stages separate, tested and refuted in Section 3.5. The released
prefix carries almost no information about 𝑑, measured in Section 4.3, and that flatness is what
empties the information channel. The reservation cancels out of the punctuality probability once
the start time is fixed, derived in Section 5.4 and strengthened in Appendices I–K.

3. Problem 1: State and Observation Reliability (Part I)
Data insight. The state question is a bookkeeping question before it is a learning question.
Of 66070 time-stamped frames from 69 recordings in 24 environments, 95.76% survive the
retention funnel, and the survivors are unevenly distributed across formats: two EgoStandard
recordings carry almost every flagged frame, while EgoPro and EgoDemo are close to clean.
Any model that reports one pooled number therefore hides the only failure the reliability weight
exists to catch. The first modelling decision is what to do with those two recordings; we keep
them and model the flag instead of deleting it.

3.2 Solution
     The pipeline is a chain of scripts, each writing one artefact (Figure 2). build_panel.py
assembles the panel (69 recordings, 66070 frames, 66070 usable frames after the funnel),
p1_state.py computes the state and the geometric descriptors, p1_model.py fits the two
reliability routes, runs the threshold sweep, the segmentation comparison and a 2 × 2 ablation
of feature set against clustering method, and check_no_peek.py asserts that no record appears
in two splits. State construction is linear in frames, the agglomeration quadratic per record; the
segment count is chosen by BIC on every third frame of 𝐾 ∈ [2, 8], and the agglomeration is
constrained so that two frames can only merge when their clusters are adjacent in time.

3.3 The state layer: representative point, stages and retention
      The continuous layer is anchored on one representative point per hand. We compared three
candidates and chose the palm centre, the mean of keypoints 0, 5, 9, 13, 17. It is the most rigid
inside a record: within-record scatter 1.46e-05 cm against 1.538 cm for the fingertip centroid,
which moves with the fingers by construction. The choice is not cosmetic even so, because the
fingertip centroid sits away from the palm centre by roughly the size of the object handled. Ap-
pendix N refits both learners under all three anchors: the segmenter’s advantage at the fingertips
does not clear its own refit spread, while the detector loses most of its average precision there.
      The discrete layer is derived by rule, not learned. A stage is a run of at least three frames,
entered when the wrist speed crosses 0.1 m/s and left when it falls below 0.05 m/s, with grasp and
release marked by an aperture change of 0.15 and an arrange stage by a closed hand that dwells
for 0.8 s. On the pooled data the shares are reach 65.68%, transport 17.25%, pick 7.24%, arrange
5.13% and place 4.70%, with mean segment 60.1 frames. The transition matrix concentrates on
three moves (reach to pick 0.7911, transport to place 0.6942 and place back to reach 0.9507),
and the conditional entropy of the next stage is 0.8022 bits against 2.0484 bits unconditionally,
Team CSWBMCM2634892                                                                           Page 10 of 25


so the sequence carries 1.2462 bits of memory. It is not a Markov chain, and a model that treats
the stage as independent per frame throws that memory away.

3.6 Discussion: where the state model fails
     Three failures are structural. The reliability weight does not transfer across environments:
the gap between 0.528 and 0.938 means a target environment needs on the order of 10 to twice
that many calibration frames before the calibrated route is usable, and without them only the rank
should be trusted. Stage agreement depends on the segment count, and segments shorter than
two seconds are systematically merged; an explicit duration prior is out of scope here. Finally,
the head-frame normalisation is not an unconditional gain: alone it lowers ARI slightly, and it
pays only when combined with the chain constraint.

3.7 Summary
     Part I delivers a 32-dimensional per-hand state in two layers, a soft reliability weight esti-
mated both with and without labels, and a reproducible baseline for each half of the question.
The state and the weight are the inputs to Part II.

4. Problem 2: Prediction under Missing Observations (Part II)
Data insight. The prediction question is decided by what the released prefix contains, not by
the capacity of the predictor. A mask of length 𝐿 hides the last 𝐿 seconds before the target; at the
shortest mask hold-last already reads 2.00 cm and at the longest it reads 6.72 cm, so the signal
decays smoothly rather than abruptly. The practical question is therefore where on that curve a
paid observation stops being worth its cost, and the answer has to be read off the same windows
that define the mask. We report the full curve rather than a single operating point, because the
curve is what the decision layer consumes.

4.4 Discussion: the net effect of looking again
      The headline of this part is negative, and it is measured on the scheduling side, not asserted:
buying observations does not pay. The observation-free policy of Part III reaches 0.8153 in S2
and 0.7406 in S3. The variant that spends a cost unit and a scenario second on a review before
each job reaches 0.8472 and 0.7500, and the paired intervals 0.0319±0.0338 and 0.0094±0.0357
both cross zero. A look costs 1 cost unit and 1 s of scenario time, and the duration error it removes
is of the same order, so the ledger balances and the sign is decided by the tie-break. The longest
prefix does not even give the best estimate: duration MAE is 2.939 s at the free prefix, 2.972 s

a property of the features, not of the estimator, and it is why the review gate in Part III almost
never fires.
      The other failure condition is the economic one. The optimum at 0.30 s belongs to our cost
model, so we re-solve the priced rule on 100 coefficient triples: the optimum stays interior in 56
of them, spans 0.20 to 1.00 s, and moves 0.45 s across the charge for a look while the per-second
term moves it by 0.00 s. The recommendation is a range, the free prefix to the earliest paid
review, not a point.

4.5 Summary
      Part II fixes a masking protocol, compares four predictors and the three named baselines on
it, prices the look, and concludes that prediction substitutes for observation only up to roughly
the crossover horizon. Beyond that, the honest action is to observe again, not to extrapolate.

5. Problem 3: Stochastic Scheduling (Part III)
Data insight. The scheduling instances are small in job count and tight in capacity: 8, 12 and
16 jobs against horizons of 100, 100 and 125 seconds. Almost every loss comes from a handful
of jobs whose predicted completion lands just after their due time, so the interesting quantity is

not the mean lateness but the shape of the on-time distribution. That shape is what a reservation
moves, and it is why the policy layer reports weighted on-time rate rather than tardiness: two
policies can have the same mean lateness and very different on-time rates.

infeasible instances the budget and horizon create, and its answer would depend on that fallback;
the rule has no solver, so no fallback, and every reported trial is admissible by construction.
      The five published mappings are the stress test, not the target. The nominal map is 𝛼 = 1,
𝛽 = 0.5. The other four move 𝛼 to 0.75 or 1.5 and 𝛽 to 0 or 1. A policy that assumed the
nominal map would be graded on a different problem in four of the five settings, which is why
the reported policy estimates the map inside the trial instead of assuming it. That estimation is
exact and cheap: because 𝑝 is affine in 𝑑, two executed jobs whose observed durations differ
determine 𝛼 and 𝛽 by solving a two-by-two system, with no data beyond the trial itself.
      Cases are 8, 12 and 16 jobs with horizons 100, 100 and 125 s and budgets 32, 34 and 40.
The review limit is 4, 6 and 8. Every trial draws durations from the official eligible intervals
by uniform sampling within family, and every policy sees the same 30 seeds 2026111700 to
2026111729, so comparisons are paired by construction. The objective is a vector (weight-

completion, weighted on-time rate, weighted tardiness, cost, reviews, elapsed time, overrun,
idle and censoring) not a scalar; our policy orders it lexicographically, taking weighted on-time
rate ahead of cost, and that ordering is our convention, not the statement’s.

admissible only if the release, cost and horizon recurrences hold at every step, which is the valid
protocol flag the statement asks for. The audit in Appendix L checks it on all 450 trials and finds
no violation. All policies share the sampler, the seeds and the cap, so differences are attributable
to the decision rule and to nothing else.

5.4 The closed form behind the comparison
     The scheduler sets the realised service time to 𝑝 = 𝛼𝑑 + 𝛽 and calls a job on time when 𝑠 + 𝑝
does not exceed its due date. Solving that inequality for the unknown duration turns punctuality
into an event on a single scalar:

                          due − 𝑠 − 𝛽                                      due − 𝑠 − 𝛽
     on time ⇐⇒ 𝑑 ≤                   ,     𝑃(on time | 𝑠, due, 𝛼) = 𝐹𝑑                  ,   (11)

where 𝐹𝑑 is the law of the source duration within the family. Nothing else in the protocol enters.
Three consequences follow.
      The reservation is absent from (11), but only conditional on 𝑠. The quantity 𝑏 postpones
the next start, 𝑡 = 𝑠 + max( 𝑝, 𝑏), so it buys capacity, not punctuality for the job it guards, while
moving every later start: a slack rule changes which jobs finish in time, not when a given job
finishes, and a reservation that is too large still costs capacity later, which is the mechanism
behind the reversal in Section 6.
      𝛼 enters only through the scale 1/𝛼, so the mapping-sensitivity table is one curve 𝐹𝑑 read
at three abscissae. Table 10 in Appendix D checks this on the per-job log: across three cases and
three multipliers the formula tracks the realised rate within 0.0397 on average and 0.0614 in the
worst cell.
      Observation can only pay through 𝐹𝑑 . One more prefix is worth something only if it moves
the law, and Part II measured that movement to be below the cost of the request. Under (11) that
is the condition for re-observing to lose value, which is the value-of-information argument of
[8] in this protocol.

train-to-validation shift in the duration pool, the rest the selection induced by executing only jobs
that can still start.

6. Empirical Decision Limits of the Task
      Two quantities bound Part III: what the instance allows with 𝑝 known, and what our infor-
mation allows. Appendix N separates them by running the reservation rule with and without the
true duration. Handing the rule the true duration lifts S2 from 0.8667 to 0.9000 and S3 from

6.1 Sensitivity of the two decision layers
      The predictor layer is refitted under four feature subsets and four context lengths, holding
the mask and the split fixed. No subset moves the error by more than a rounding step, and
the context length moves it by less than a tenth of a centimetre across five to forty frames; the
binding constraint is the released prefix, not the feature set. The conformal radius is more fragile:
it grows by an order of magnitude between the shortest and the longest mask, so the paper reports
the interval rather than a point.
      The rule layer is tested two ways. One-at-a-time moves of the seven rule knobs change the
on-time rate by at most 5.22 points in Case S2 and 6.25 in Case S3, and the slack coefficient
and quantile shift have exactly zero effect under the selected rules because those rules never read
them. A full 35 design over the five knobs that do matter shows a curved, almost everywhere
concave surface: every knob away from its frozen value loses more than a linear reading predicts,
Team CSWBMCM2634892                                                                                                       Page 25 of 25


and no pair restores the loss. The frozen rule is therefore an interior optimum of a curved surface,
not a lucky corner of a flat one. Appendix K reports both designs in full.

7. Model Evaluation
     Appendices D, G and L score us against pre-declared marks and name the three that were
not met.

7.1 What the marks measured
      Three pre-declared marks were missed and each is reported with its own table. The quantile
predictor’s longest mask reaches 6.14 cm against a mark of 5.8 and a ninth-decile radius of
9.97 cm against 9.5, although at the shortest mask the same family halves the ridge error to
1.08 cm, so the regression is a horizon effect, not a fitting failure. Of 90 audited review cells, 0
clear the one-point gate: best 0.28, worst -0.56, the paid arm fires in 35 cells, and a free perfect
prefix of six extra frames is worth two and a half points while a paid one is worth less than
its own cost. We keep all three in the score sheet because a paper that reports only the marks
it clears cannot be checked. Appendix N states each reversal condition and the three places
leakage is controlled, and discloses the one threat it cannot control: a single labeller on the
reference boundaries.

7.2 Strengths and weaknesses
     The reliability weight crosses environments only after recalibration, stage agreement is
stable once BIC fixes the count, prediction pays only to the crossover horizon, and the ranking
puts weighted on-time ahead. One gap stays open: the Part II conformal radius never becomes
the Part III reservation quantile.

8. Conclusion
      Three answers, one per question. A two-layer hand state with a soft reliability weight is
recoverable and transfers across environments only after local calibration, though as a training
weight it moves prediction by millimetres. Prediction substitutes for observation to a quarter-
second crossover, and free waiting beats every paid request. A slack-aware policy with an ex-
plicit reservation quantile raises the weighted on-time rate over EDF in both capacity-constrained
cases, and the truth is worth three to four points once the rule is fixed: punctuality is bought by
the rule, not by the data.

def ward_segments(X, K):
    n = len(X)
    if n < 3 or K < 2:
        return np.zeros(n, int)

    C = sparse.diags([np.ones(n - 1), np.ones(n - 1)], [1, -1], shape=(n, n),
   format="csr")
    lab = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=K, connectivity=C,
   linkage="ward").fit_predict(X)
    # remove isolated flickers, then relabel contiguous runs
    for _ in range(2):
        for i in range(1, len(lab) - 1):
            if lab[i] != lab[i - 1] and lab[i] != lab[i + 1]:
                  lab[i] = lab[i - 1]
    _, runs = np.unique(lab, return_inverse=True)
    # merge non-adjacent repeats of same label by run id
    run_id = np.zeros(len(lab), int); m = 0
    for i in range(1, len(lab)):
        if lab[i] != lab[i - 1]:
            m += 1
        run_id[i] = m
    return run_id

def windows(split, L, cap=CAP):
    out = []
    for r in recs[recs.split == split].rec:
        ii = np.where(rec == r)[0]
        F = hand_of(r)[ii, :3]

        ok = ~bad
        run = 0

            if run >= CTX + L:

Why temperature scaling fails on the reliability route
      The temporal route shows the failure in sharper form. Its mean predicted badness is 0.2296
against a base rate of 0.0190. Temperature scaling (𝑇 = 2.66) raises the Brier score from 0.1341
to 0.1536 and the calibration error from 0.2106 to 0.3447, and even Platt scaling (0.0217) loses
to a constant predictor at 0.0187. The cause is arithmetic: a temperature only rescales the logit,
while the mis-fit is an intercept, so no positive temperature maps that mean onto a base rate two
orders of magnitude smaller.

I Three propositions and the seven tests between them
      The body reports seven negative results in seven places. One at a time they look like failures;
together they are one proposition tested seven times. This appendix states it, proves it, and lines
the tests up against it; two further propositions do the same for the reservation and for the choice
of its level.

Proposition 1 (the information channel)
         Within the protocol’s action set and information set, the data enter the objective
         only through the duration law 𝐹𝑑 and the release sequence. Any change that leaves
         the posterior of the source duration 𝑑 unchanged is, in expectation, the identity.

Proof. (i) The realisation is deterministic. Given a draw (𝑑1 , . . . , 𝑑𝑛 ) and a release sequence,
the service time 𝑝 𝑗 = 𝛼𝑑 𝑗 + 𝛽 is fixed, and start and completion times follow from the policy’s
decisions and 𝑠 𝑗+1 ≥ 𝑠 𝑗 + max( 𝑝 𝑗 , 𝑏 𝑗 ); the weighted on-time rate is therefore a deterministic
function of (𝑑, releases, 𝜋), with no other random source. (ii) The data have one entrance. A
policy sees only 𝑜1:𝑡 . Write 𝑞 𝑡 (𝑑) = 𝑃(𝑑 | 𝑜1:𝑡 ) for the posterior. If an intervention leaves
𝑞 𝑡 unchanged for every 𝑡 and every task family, the policy sees the same information at every
step, takes the same decisions, and reproduces the same rate on every draw, hence the same
expectation. (iii) Sufficiency. When the prefix is uninformative, 𝑞 𝑡 ≡ 𝐹𝑑 , the optimal policy
depends only on the prior and on the release and due sequence. More features then have no
entrance: the channel is absent, not narrow.

The seven tests
    Table 14 in Appendix E is the audit trail. Each row is an intervention a reviewer would
expect to help; each changes the posterior of 𝑑 by less than the measurement noise: the missing-
frame channel (local fill, interpolation), reliability scores as sample weights, self-supervised

pre-training, a longer released prefix, a paid second observation, a deeper sequence model, and
the motion features of all 184 videos. The seventh row is the sharpest, because it has a control:
permuting the video columns reproduces the gain that the video block appeared to buy, so the
block was a wider design matrix, not information.
     The upper bound is measured the same way. Giving the policy the true duration inside the
same rule is worth 3.33 points in S2 and 3.85 points in S3 — what perfect information about 𝑑
is worth here; every feature-side intervention is bounded above by it, and none approaches it.

Proposition 2 (reservation neutrality)
      Given the start time 𝑠, the on-time probability of a job is 𝑃(𝐶 ≤ due | 𝑠) =
                           
      𝐹𝑑 (due − 𝑠 − 𝛽)/𝛼 , independent of the reservation 𝑏. The reservation acts on
      the next job through 𝑠′ = 𝑠 + max( 𝑝, 𝑏) and on nothing else.

Proof. 𝐶 = 𝑠 + 𝑝 = 𝑠 + 𝛼𝑑 + 𝛽 ≤ due if and only if 𝑑 ≤ (due − 𝑠 − 𝛽)/𝛼; the right-hand side
does not contain 𝑏, so the probability is 𝐹𝑑 evaluated there.
     The condition on 𝑠 cannot be dropped. If 𝑏 > 𝑝 the next job starts later, its own (due′ − 𝑠′ −
𝛽)/𝛼 shrinks, and its on-time probability falls: the reservation trades this job’s capacity for the
next job’s time. In the most loaded case raising the reserve from bottom to top costs about ten
points of weighted on-time rate (Section 5.3), while under the nominal mapping, where 𝑏 ≤ 𝑝
throughout, all policies sit at the same rate with reserved idle near zero.

Proposition 3 (reservation dominance)
      Fix a policy whose choice of job does not depend on the reserve, and fix the duration
      realisation. If 𝑏′ ≤ 𝑏, then every start time under 𝑏′ is no later than under 𝑏, every
      completion is no later, and every cost is no larger. Hence the smallest admissible
      reserve weakly dominates every larger one; with the protocol floor 𝑏 = 0.2, 𝑏★ = 𝑏.

Proof. Order the executed jobs by start. The first start 𝑠1 is fixed by the release sequence; its
completion 𝐶1 = 𝑠1 + 𝑝 1 contains no 𝑏. The next start is 𝑠2 = 𝑠1 + max( 𝑝 1 , 𝑏 1 ), which is non-
decreasing in 𝑏 1 ; by induction every later start and completion is non-decreasing in the whole
reserve vector. A completion that is on time under 𝑏 therefore remains on time under 𝑏′ ≤ 𝑏,
so the weighted on-time set can only grow, while 𝑐 = 2 + 0.1𝑏 + 0.2𝐷 can only fall and the
feasibility test 𝑠 + 𝑏 ≤ 𝐻 can only become easier. Taking expectations over the duration law
preserves the order. If 𝑏 ≤ 𝑝 for every job, then max( 𝑝, 𝑏) = 𝑝 and the bound is exactly flat; the
plateau and the fall are the two branches of the same statement.

observation is worth no more than zero; and adjusting the reservation is worth zero conditional

What the video actually buys
     A video branch answers the obvious objection to every table above: the dataset contains
178,512 decoded frames from 184 videos, and this paper otherwise reads only the released key-
points. The earlier duration test found nothing because duration is a record-level target: the
camera cannot see how long a job will take. Occlusion is different. It is a frame-level visual
event, and the camera sees it directly. We therefore score the same detector twice, once from
hand geometry alone and once with twelve camera statistics chosen on the training split only and
standardised within each record, so that a bright room or a dark room does not become a label.
Table 28 gives the result. The geometry-only arm reaches AP 0.0516 and AUC 0.7422; adding
the video statistics moves these to AP 0.0681 and AUC 0.7694, a relative AP gain of 31.9 per
cent and an AUC gain of 0.0271. The pre-registered bar was 10 per cent AP and 0.010 AUC;
both are cleared. Video alone is precise but poorly ranked (AP 0.0596, AUC 0.6152), so the
honest claim is a fusion gain, not a replacement of pose. This is the one place in the paper where
an additional observation channel pays, and it pays exactly where the protocol says it should: on
the reliability of a frame, not on the length of a job.

A stronger test: video inside the production detector
      The fusion above compares against a deliberately small geometry baseline. A second test
asks whether the same statistics help the production detector. We append all 120 video statistics
to the V8 input and retrain under the identical protocol. Test AP falls from 0.1494 to 0.0581,
and the weakest-environment AUC falls from 0.7777 to 0.5247. The result is a clean negative:
the camera carries reliability information that a small detector can use, but the strong pose de-
tector already extracts that information from geometry, and extra capacity only overfits the 131
s training budget.

Sampling rate and late fusion
     Two follow-up checks decide where the video branch belongs. First, re-extracting the same
184 videos at 10 Hz instead of 2 Hz raises the feature table from 12,020 to 59,864 rows, but it
does not help: the video-only arm falls to AP 0.0269 (from 0.0596 at 2 Hz) and the fused arm
reaches AP 0.0559 with AUC 0.7397, so the pre-registered gate fails. The 2 Hz rate is the useful
one; ten hertz mostly repeats the same frame and adds correlated noise. Second, score-level late
fusion with the production detector settles the stronger question. With the blend weight chosen
on validation AP, the fused test AP is 0.16961 against 0.16961 for V8 alone, a relative change of
0.0008 per cent, and AUC changes by +0.00002. A detector that already sees the hands does not
need the camera; a detector that sees only a weak geometric summary does. That is the precise
sense in which the video channel is useful, and it is the sense the earlier duration test missed.

What the re-scoring changed
     Four statements in the paper changed after this audit. First, the number 0.5487 that earlier
drafts called a published baseline is an internal replication of our own bi-TCN arm; the same rule
reproduces at 0.5355 in a second run, a gap of 0.0132 that we have not explained. We now call it
an internal reference line and report both runs. Second, the Part I headline is a distribution rather
than a point: the shipped protocol, retrained 3 times from scratch, gives ARI 0.5366 ± 0.0124
(range 0.5293–0.5509). The first-draft value 0.5487 sits near the top of that range, and decoding
the weights we retained gave 0.4902, so the point estimate was a favourable draw rather than a
stable property of the method; the gap to the agglomerative baseline, 12.28 points on the refit
mean, survives the spread. Third, P2 is reported on the mean-distance metric in this appendix,
while the RMSE table in the body remains as a second, stricter view. Fourth, the P3 protocol
audit adds the two legality fields that the first round omitted, and both are clean over 450 trials.
None of these changes makes a result larger; each makes it harder to over-read.

mask window, since the residual there is dominated by motion the context cannot see rather than by the occlusion
we remove. A paid second look would pay where its delay is shorter than the wait it replaces; the crossover curve
in Section 4.1 locates that boundary for us.

O Validation notes
Validation notes moved from the body
Part I. The split is by environment, so every test number here is a cross-environment number, and the
threshold sweep reports retention, recall and precision on one curve, which makes the precision col-
lapse at high thresholds a stated property, not a surprise. Stressing the two test environments separately
(Appendix N): logistics_x_courier_station_0011 holds 12634 frames with 426 positives and
logistics_x_courier_station_0041 7274 frames with 36, scoring 0.528 and 0.938. The pre-declared
mark was AUC above 0.55 with average precision above prevalence: the pooled split passes, the courier-station
environment does not.
Part II. Validation has three legs. The predictors are compared on identical windows, so the comparison is paired
by construction. Coverage is checked against the nominal level, and the pass mark is coverage of at least 90% minus
a tolerance of five points. The crossover is checked against an independent probe with different sampling, which
agrees at the middle horizons and disagrees at the shortest one; the two curves are reported separately and never
merged. The economic conclusion is re-run over a grid of cost coefficients, which is why Section 4.4 quotes a range,
not a single optimum. Conditional coverage at the longest official prefix ranges from 0.839 to 1.000 across the 4 test
environments, against a marginal 0.893; one environment falls below 0.85 (1 of 4). The marginal guarantee does
not promise the former, and with one to three test records per environment a per-environment conformal calibration
cannot be estimated, so we report the spread as a limitation instead of calibrating four radii.
Part III. The paired comparison is the primary evidence: every policy sees the same durations on the same seed,
so seed noise cancels. The interval against the reference excludes zero in both informative cases, so the ordering is
not seed luck. Against RISK, the variant that spends reviews, the interval 0.0319±0.0338 and 0.0094±0.0357 covers
zero, so paying for observations is not a gain this experiment can detect. The fused-predictor variant is worse than
the plain one in S2 by -0.0500 and indistinguishable in S3, a warning about stacking models, not a result about
scheduling.
       Two secondary checks bound the claim. The first is the mapping, and Table 5 puts it on the record: across
the five published mappings the reference moves by 40.3 points in S2 and 42.1 in S3, while the frozen rule moves
by 30.7 and 30.2. No scheduler-only change in this paper moves the rate by that much, so the mapping stays in the
paper as the dominant factor, and the gain of the rule is quoted inside a mapping and never across one. It is not
uniform: the mean paired gain is 5.5 and 7.6 points, the weakest cell gains 0.0 and 2.2, and 1 cell in S2 is saturated,
where the reference already reaches the rule.
