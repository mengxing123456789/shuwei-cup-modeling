Problem D：A Model for the Sole Centrism of a Stellar
System and Closures of Planetary Orbits
i.
Scientific Problem
After Kepler formulated his three laws of planetary motion in 1609, Isaac Newton
established the law of universal gravitation, which provided a unified framework of the
motion of all planets, thereby laying a solid foundation for the development of modern
astrophysics. However, as observational techniques have become increasingly precise,
along with the synergistic effects of Newtonian gravitational dynamics in multi-body
systems and the spacetime curvature predicted by general relativity, Kepler's laws have
been found to have significant deficiencies in both accuracy and rigor.
Coordinate system
First, the orbital observational data used by Kepler is incomplete in the spatial
dimension：It is apparent that any planetary body executes three-dimensional motion;
determining true orbit requires observations from at least three mutually orthogonal
directions—corresponding, in engineering terms, to a three-view orthographic
representation. In the era of Galileo, Tycho and Kepler, however, observations were

obtained from only a single terrestrial viewpoint: a relatively fixed line of sight lying
in the plane of the ecliptic. No additional viewpoints were available, particularly a top-
view observation perpendicular to the ecliptic.
Consequently, the data on which Kepler relied suffers from insufficient
observability of the planetary system state, imposing fundamental limitations on the
complete reconstruction of the celestial-system state. Second, the observational data
were also temporally incomplete. The temporal baseline accumulated by Tycho was
insufficient; the principal observations were concentrated in the interval from 1580 to
1600. Complete data establishing completion of an orbital cycle were therefore neither
observed nor obtained, and a full cycle of the radial periodic perturbations was never
captured. The actual radial resonant period may be extremely long (possibly several
centuries). From the standpoint of system identification in control theory, a finite-
duration time-series sample is inadequate for fully identifying long-period modes.
Third, idealization is inherent in the two-body problem. The strictly elliptical (or
circular) orbits derived from the inverse-square law of gravitation constitute an exact
solution to a mathematically integrable two-body conservative system. A real planetary
system, however, is a non-integrable multi-body dissipative system (even if dissipation
is extremely small) that exhibits chaos and resonance. An elliptical (or circular) orbit is
therefore essentially a reduced-order idealized reference model rather than a complete
description of the dynamics of the true multi-body nonlinear system.
Fourth, a sufficient condition does not constitute a necessary-and-sufficient
conclusion. Deriving and proving the law of universal gravitation starting from circular
motion and elliptical orbits could not exclude other non-conic or composite-curve orbits;
alternative orbital models may equally well describe the actual trajectories. Therefore,
this argument represents a derivation based on a particular sufficient condition rather
than a necessary-and-sufficient identification of the structural parameters of the
planetary dynamical system [1-7].
