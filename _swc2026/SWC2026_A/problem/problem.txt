for Multi-Fluid Injection Development
in Deep Unconventional Reservoirs
China’s recoverable tight-oil reserves are approximately 17.82 billion tonnes,
accounting for about 40% of its recoverable petroleum resources, and are mainly
distributed in the Ordos, Junggar, Sichuan, and Songliao basins; they constitute an
important replacement resource for safeguarding national energy security. In deep
tight-oil reservoirs, the matrix permeability is generally no higher than

productivity of a single well is low, so that the recovery factor under conventional
development is commonly below 10%.
In field practice, a composite fluid of liquid/supercritical CO2 plus a
nano-imbibition agent is used for reservoir stimulation and huff-and-puff
development. The process comprises three stages: (1) high-pressure injection, in
which CO2 and the nano-imbibition agent are injected into the formation in a
specified mixing ratio and hydraulic fractures are created under conditions above the
formation breakdown pressure; (2) shut-in soaking, during which CO2 diffuses into
the matrix and dissolves in the crude oil and formation water, the nano-agent alters
the oil–water interfacial tension and wettability, and the reservoir pressure, effective
stress, and damage state are reconfigured accordingly; and (3) well opening and
production, in which crude oil and the mixed fluid are produced.
Soaking time is the key variable linking “stimulation performance” to “economic
viability”: if the soak is too short, CO2 has not yet penetrated deep into the matrix and
the nano-agent has not had time to act fully, so the stimulation extent is limited; if the
soak is too long, operation and capital-occupancy costs continue to rise and
production is deferred, so the net present value (NPV) may decline. The injection
composition, injection rate, total injection volume, and injection timing also affect

both the production-enhancement response and the operation cost, and must be
weighed quantitatively within a unified modeling framework.
Injection timing τinj: after fracturing is completed, injection of the CO2 +
nanoparticle composite fluid begins only after a waiting period of τinj days; τinj = 0
means that the fluid is injected immediately after fracturing ends. This definition is
supplemented in the notes to Table A.3.
Problem 1
During high-pressure fluid injection and soaking, fluid seepage,
rock deformation, pore-pressure variation, and damage evolution occur
simultaneously within the reservoir. Establish the coupled equations for the
interacting seepage, stress, and damage fields, comprising the fluid-seepage continuity
equation, the rock mechanical constitutive relation, the Biot effective-stress coupling
relation, and the damage evolution equation. Specify the physical assumptions, initial
conditions, and boundary conditions of the model, briefly explain the physical
meaning of each equation, and distinguish the boundary conditions of the injection,
soaking, and production stages.
Problem 2
To identify the key factors governing reservoir stimulation,
nondimensionalize the coupled equations established in Problem 1, obtain the
dimensionless governing parameter groups, and explain the physical meaning of each
parameter; with reference to Appendix A, give the order of magnitude of a reasonable
value for each parameter. Use dimensional analysis to derive an approximate
analytical relation between the soaking time and the radius of stimulation influence,
and analyze which dimensionless parameters significantly modify the effect of
soaking time on the stimulation extent.
Problem 3
Formulate a multi-objective optimization model with the objectives
of enhancing reservoir production and reducing the total operational cost, define the
design variables, and impose engineering constraints on the basis of equipment
capacity, the limits on nanomaterial use, and the upper limit of formation
pressure-bearing capacity. Taking Case0 (the base case) as the starting point, select
three representative scenarios—a low-permeability, high-stress reservoir; a
medium-permeability, low-stress reservoir; and a high-permeability reservoir with

complex natural fractures—choose an appropriate intelligent optimization algorithm,
and solve for the Pareto-optimal solution set by means of simulation or a surrogate
model. Justify the choice of algorithm, and provide 2–3 compromise operational
schemes for reference for each of the three scenarios. In view of measurement errors
in geological parameters, assume ±15% random perturbations in key parameters such
as permeability and in-situ stress, screen the Pareto-optimal set for robust operational
schemes with strong disturbance resistance, and give engineering recommendations.
Problem 4
Use the productivity simulation data in Attachment C to build a
productivity prediction model, divide the training and test sets appropriately, and
adopt corresponding measures to prevent overfitting. Using the economic parameters
in Appendix B, calculate the net present value (NPV), plot the variation of NPV with
soaking time, and determine the optimal soaking time for each of the three scenarios.
Compare the results of the data-driven model with those of the mechanistic model;
where discrepancies arise, analyze their causes in terms of model assumptions,
numerical error, and data noise. Construct a comprehensive evaluation index system
for reservoir stimulation performance, carry out a global sensitivity analysis of the
key geological and operational parameters, and identify the parameters that most
strongly influence the optimal soaking time.
Supplementary notes:
Stimulation radius: defined as the farthest normal distance, measured from the
fracture surface, at which a specified physical quantity reaches a given threshold. The
quantities that may be specified include the pore-pressure disturbance Δp, the CO2
mass fraction, the nanoparticle concentration, and the damage variable d; the
recommended thresholds are Δp/p0 = 5%, a CO2 mass fraction of 0.01, a nanoparticle
concentration of 0.01 wt%, and d = 0.10. Unless otherwise stated, the term refers by
default to the normal distance at which the pressure disturbance reaches 5% of p0.
Problems 2, 3, and 4 must remain consistent with Problem 1.
Model Assumptions and Unified Conventions
To facilitate the formulation of the equations, the following unified conventions
are adopted. Contestants may relax, replace, or supplement them, provided that the

reasons are stated and consistency is maintained across all problems.
Definition of the three fields: The three fields referred to in the problem
statement are uniformly taken to be the seepage field, the stress field, and the damage
field. The unknowns of the seepage field are the pore pressure, the phase saturations,
and the component concentrations; those of the stress field are the displacement, the
strain, and the effective stress; and those of the damage field are the scalar damage
variable d together with the porosity and permeability evolution driven by it. The pore
pressure is an unknown of the seepage field and also enters the stress field through the
Biot effective stress; it is therefore the coupling variable between the two and is no
longer listed separately as an independent pore-pressure field. The expression
“seepage field–in-situ stress field–pore pressure field” in the background is to be
understood in this sense.
Basic Assumptions
(1) The reservoir is an isothermal, isotropic continuum, and the matrix is
described by a single-porosity formulation; the hydraulic fracture is a
high-conductivity planar region embedded in the matrix; natural fractures may be
superimposed in Case3.
(2) Under reservoir conditions, CO2 is in a supercritical state (Tc = 304.13 K, pc
= 7.38 MPa). The density and viscosity of CO2 vary with pressure and temperature
and are described by the Peng–Robinson equation or by the property table in
Appendix A; the latent heat of phase change is neglected.
(3) The dissolution of CO2 in crude oil and formation water is treated as
instantaneous equilibrium described by Henry’s law; dissolution causes crude-oil
swelling and viscosity reduction. Whether the free CO2 phase is modeled separately is
left to the contestants; if it is neglected, the applicable conditions must be stated and a
comparison with the case in which the free phase is considered must be provided.
(4) The nanoparticles are transported only in the aqueous phase, and advection,
molecular diffusion, and reversible adsorption are taken into account; the
nanoparticles influence the capillary pressure and relative permeability by reducing
the oil–water interfacial tension and altering the contact angle.

(5) The rock is an isotropic poroelastic body, and the small-deformation and
quasi-static assumptions are adopted; damage is described by the scalar variable d,
and after damage the Young’s modulus, porosity, and permeability are reduced
according to the prescribed relations.
(6) Flow obeys Darcy’s law, and the relative permeability and capillary pressure
are functions of saturation; stress sensitivity is represented through the variation of
porosity, matrix permeability, fracture aperture, and fracture permeability with
effective stress (or pore pressure).
(7) The top and bottom caprocks are impermeable, and the outer boundaries are
constant-pressure/constant-stress boundaries; gravity, the thermal field, chemical
reactions, salt precipitation, asphaltene precipitation, and wettability hysteresis are
neglected; back-injection into production wells is not permitted.
(8) The economic evaluation is carried out strictly in accordance with Appendix
B; all injection volumes, production volumes, and material consumptions are
measured at the reference state of 25 MPa and 300 K; taxes and fees, salvage value,
and common sunk costs are not considered.

B.2
Nanomaterial and CO2 Consumption
Let the total volume of the injected composite fluid at the reference state of 25
MPa and 300 K be Vinj, with a CO2 volume fraction of fCO2; then
VCO2 = fCO2Vinj,
Vaq = (1 - fCO2)Vinj
The masses of CO2 and of the nanomaterial are, respectively (in units of t)
mCO2 = ρCO2,refVCO2/1000,
mnano = (Cn/100)ρaqVaq/1000
where ρCO2,ref = 850 kg/m3, ρaq = 1000 kg/m3, and Cn is substituted in wt%. The
corresponding costs are
Cnano = cnanomnano,
CCO2 = cCO2mCO2
The upper limit of nanomaterial consumption is 30 t per well treatment; schemes
exceeding this limit are regarded as infeasible. The nanomaterial consumption is
based on the volume Vaq of the injected nano-agent aqueous solution and is not
converted from the stimulated reservoir volume; if another convention of preparing
the fluid according to the stimulated volume is adopted, the conversion relation
between Vaq and the stimulated volume must be given and kept consistent throughout

the paper.
B.3
Total Cost, Cash Flow, and NPV
The total cost (undiscounted, including construction and production operating
costs) is
Ctotal = Cfrac + cinjtinj + csoakts + Cnano + CCO2 + cprodQo + cwaterQw
where tinj = Vinj/qinj is the actual injection duration, and Qo and Qw are the
cumulative oil production and water production over 360 d of production, respectively.
The net cash flow on day t is
CF(t) = Poil*Qo - cprodQo - cwaterQw - cinjIinj - csoakIsoak - cnanomnanoday - cCO2mCO2day
where Iinj(t) and Isoak(t) are indicator functions for the injection and soaking
stages, and mnanoday(t) and mCO2day(t) are the consumptions of the nano-agent and CO2
on that day (t/d). The NPV is
NPV = -Cfrac + Σt=1T CF(t)/(1+r)t/365
For a candidate scheme, T = tinj + ts + 360 d; for the baseline scheme B1
(fracturing only, without injection of the composite fluid), T = 360 d; the incremental
net present value is ΔNPV = NPV −NPVB1. The discounting reference is day 0, with
discounting applied at the end of each day; during the production stage no daily
operation charge is counted, and only the variable production cost and the
produced-water treatment cost are included; the daily soaking charge is included, and
the daily injection charge is counted for the actual number of injection days.
