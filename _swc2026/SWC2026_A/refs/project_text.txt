                                         Summary
   Deep tight oil reservoirs are developed by multi-fracture horizontal wells; a CO2 -assisted
nanofluid huff-and-puff treatment adds a soak stage between injection and production, and the
field question is how long to soak and how much to inject. This paper builds and verifies a cou-
pled seepage–stress–damage model for a single fracture–matrix block, derives its dimensionless
structure, optimises the treatment design, and calibrates a data-driven production model that is
used for a net-present-value (NPV) analysis of the three design cases supplied with the problem.
   The mechanistic model is a one-dimensional radial system normal to the fracture face, with
Biot effective stress [1], stress-sensitive permeability [4], damage, CO2 dissolution and nanopar-
ticle adsorption. A finite-volume scheme with 1200 steps closes the mass balance to 7.7 × 10−5 ;
three grid levels agree to 7.7×10−6 in the 60-day radius. The computed radius follows 𝑅 𝑝 ∝ 𝑡 0.50
in every time window, with 𝑅 𝑝 (60 d) = 40.64, 85.19, 181.46 m in Cases 1–3. Ten dimension-
less groups are derived and evaluated; the accumulation term is 0.1–2.7% of the viscous drive,
gravity 0.2%, and capillary 3–6%, which justifies the pressure equation actually solved.
   A two-layer response theory resolves an apparent contradiction: the pressure front scales
as 𝑡 1/2 , while the synthetic cumulative production of Attachment C saturates exponentially in
soak time with time constants 6.7, 8.3 and 10.0 d. The data-side exponent falls to 0.22–0.34
because the measurement integrates a retention-limited effective volume, not the front. The
model reproduces the measured curves with 𝑅 2 = 0.9986–0.9998 and predicts an optimal soak
time of 10, 23.5 and 35.4 d in the three cases (25 d on the five-day design grid in Case 2).
   Economically, Case 3 is profitable (peak NPV +184.3 × 104 CNY at 𝑡 𝑠 = 35 d), whereas
Cases 1 and 2 are structurally loss-making: at the economic optimum their NPV is −720.2 and
−445.4×104 CNY, and the oil volume required for break-even is 11.5 and 2.13 times the observed
volume, or the oil price would have to reach 35,417 and 6,840 CNY/m3 . No plausible reduction
of the fracturing cost closes the gap. A global Sobol [7, 8] analysis with 18,432 evaluations
shows that the maximum NPV is dominated by the fracturing cost and the decline amplitude,
whereas the optimal soak time is controlled by the soak cost and the same amplitude; the two
rankings are consistent with an independent elasticity criterion to within 17%.
   Every number in the paper is produced by the accompanying code; each stage is checked
against an analytic limit or the official verification vector (848.25×104 CNY).

1.     Introduction

1.1 Background
Tight oil reservoirs are characterised by matrix permeability in the 0.01–0.1 mD range, so a
well must be fractured before it can produce at an industrial rate. After fracturing, the matrix
adjacent to the fracture face remains the bottleneck: the pressure disturbance penetrates only a
few tens of metres during a treatment, and the oil that can be recovered is controlled by how far,
and how effectively, that disturbance travels. Two EOR mechanisms are commonly combined in
this setting. CO2 dissolves in the oil, reduces its viscosity and swells it; nanoparticles adsorb on
the rock surface, alter wettability and reduce the interfacial resistance to flow. In a huff-and-puff
operation the two are injected together, the well is shut in for a soak period 𝑡 𝑠 , and then produced
for a fixed period (360 d in this problem).
     The design question is deceptive. A longer soak allows more CO2 to dissolve and more
nanoparticles to attach, but it also delays production and, because the discount rate is 8% per
year, it costs money every day. The problem therefore has an interior optimum, and the optimum
depends on the interaction of a diffusion [2] process (slow), an adsorption process (fast, but
saturating) and an economic discounting process. A model that treats only the diffusion [2] front
will predict that longer soaking always helps; a model that treats only the production decline will
predict that soaking never helps. Neither is adequate.


1.2 Problem statement
The competition problem asks for four things. First, a coupled seepage–stress–damage model for
the injection, soak and production stages, with initial and boundary conditions written separately
for each stage. Second, a non-dimensionalisation of that model, a table of the resulting groups
and their magnitudes, and an approximate analytic relation between soak time and the stimulated
radius; the analysis must identify which groups materially change that relation. Third, a multi-
objective optimisation of the treatment design in three geological cases, with a proxy model,
Pareto fronts, two or three compromise schemes per case, and a ±15% robustness screen on per-
meability and in-situ stress. Fourth, a production model calibrated on the 38,880-row synthetic
data set, an NPV calculation using the parameters of Appendix B, the NPV–soak-time curve and
the optimal soak time for each case, an attribution of the difference between the data-driven and
mechanistic predictions, and a global sensitivity analysis.


1.3 Contributions
The paper makes five contributions, each tied to a numbered section.

2.     Data, notation and assumptions

2.1 The data set
The supplementary file contains 38,880 daily records: three geological cases, three nanoparticle
concentrations 𝑐 𝑛 ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 1.5} wt%, twelve soak durations 𝑡 𝑠 = 5, 10, . . . , 60 d, and 360
production days. Each record gives the bottom-hole pressure, the oil rate and the cumulative
oil. Two audit results frame the subsequent analysis. First, the cumulative column equals the
cumulative sum of the daily rate to within 0.2 m3 over the whole file, which means the cumulative
column carries no independent noise; reconstructing the rate from it would return the same
numbers, not new information. Second, the residual of a smooth fit to the daily rate has a median
relative standard deviation of 9.7%, larger than the 5% noise stated in the problem, because the
curvature of the decline and the pressure variation are absorbed into the residual. We therefore
treat the stated 5% as a lower bound on the noise level, and we do not claim that any model can
fit below it.


2.2 Assumptions
The model rests on eight assumptions. Each is stated with the reason it is acceptable and the
place where it is relaxed or tested.
A1 The reservoir is isothermal. The problem supplies no thermal boundary condition, and the
     thermal expansion term is identically zero in the energy balance; the dimensionless thermal
Team ASWBMCM2634892                                                                    Page 5 of 25

     group is zero in all three cases.
A2 The matrix block is homogeneous and isotropic, with constant porosity and a permeability
     that depends on effective stress through an exponential law. This is the standard tight-oil
     idealisation; heterogeneity is represented at the design stage by the ±15% perturbation.
A3 Flow is one-dimensional in the direction normal to the fracture face. The fracture is long
     compared with the penetration depth (𝐿 𝑓 = 100–140 m versus 𝑅 𝑝 ≤ 182 m), so edge effects
     are secondary. The model is a single-fracture, single-well element.
A4 The fracture itself is infinitely conductive: the pressure at the fracture face is the bottom-
     hole pressure. This is consistent with the fracture permeability 𝑘 𝑓 = 5000 mD being four
     orders of magnitude above the matrix.
A5 CO2 dissolves instantaneously up to an equilibrium concentration given by Henry’s law; the
     dissolution is not rate-limited. The diffusion [2] of dissolved CO2 is slow and is retained
     explicitly.
A6 Nanoparticles are transported by advection and diffusion [2], and are removed from the
     mobile phase by first-order adsorption with partition coefficient 𝐾 𝑑 . The adsorption is
     reversible and linear.
A7 Damage is an irreversible reduction of elastic modulus driven by tensile strain at the fracture
     face; it is described by a scalar damage variable with a threshold and a saturating evolution
     law.
A8 The three stages are sequential and their durations are decision variables subject to 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 +𝑡 𝑠 ≤
     90 d and 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 = 𝑉𝑖𝑛 𝑗 /𝑞𝑖𝑛 𝑗 .

3.2 Initial and boundary conditions for the three stages
The three stages differ only in their boundary conditions, but the difference matters.
   • Injection (0 < 𝑡 ≤ 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 ): 𝑝(0, 𝑡) = 𝑝 𝑀 , 𝑞(0, 𝑡) = 𝑞𝑖𝑛 𝑗 , 𝑐(0, 𝑡) = 𝑐𝑖𝑛 , 𝑛(0, 𝑡) = 𝑛𝑖𝑛 ; the far
     boundary is no-flow, 𝜕 𝑝/𝜕𝑥 = 0 at 𝑥 = 𝐿.
   • Soak (𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 < 𝑡 ≤ 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 + 𝑡 𝑠 ): the well is closed, so 𝑞(0, 𝑡) = 0 and 𝜕 𝑝/𝜕𝑥 = 0 at both ends;
     the concentrations are frozen at their end-of-injection profiles and the only active process
     is diffusion [2], adsorption and stress relaxation.
   • Production (𝑡 > 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 + 𝑡 𝑠 ): 𝑝(0, 𝑡) = 𝑝 𝑤 𝑓 = 35 MPa and the oil rate is computed from the
     pressure gradient; the far boundary remains no-flow.
The initial condition is hydrostatic: 𝑝(𝑥, 0) = 𝑝 0 = 45 MPa, 𝑆 𝑤 = 0.25, 𝑐 = 0, 𝑛 = 0.

3.4 Results
Figure 2 plots the simulated radius against soak time on log–log axes; Table 1 collects the radii
and the fitted exponents. Three facts stand out.
   First, the radius follows a power law with an exponent indistinguishable from 1/2: the fitted
values are 0.5030, 0.5017 and 0.5008 for the three cases when all times are used, and the early
and late windows differ by less than 0.002. The exponent is therefore a structural property of
the diffusion [2] equation, not a fitted coincidence.

4.2 Term balance
Table 3 compares the terms of the pressure equation. The viscous drive is the reference. The
accumulation term is 2.7%, 0.6% and 0.1% of the drive in the three cases; gravity is 0.2% or
less; capillary is 6%, 4% and 3%. The thermal term is identically zero. This justifies solving
a slightly compressible pressure equation with a capillary correction, and it also tells us what
to drop: gravity can be dropped everywhere, while capillary must be retained near the fracture
face where the saturation changes fastest.

4.5 Which groups matter, in one table
The dimensionless analysis can be summarised as follows. Π1 sets the scale of the radius; Π3 and
Π8 set the enhancement and the near-face damage; Π6 sets the retention and therefore the time
constant of the second layer; Π10 = 𝑡 𝑠 /𝑇𝑝 sets the economic weight of soaking. The remaining
groups (Π2 , Π4 , Π5 , Π7 , Π9 ) are either small or enter only through a parameter that is already
measured. This is a stronger statement than a list of groups: it says which four numbers a field
engineer must measure to predict the optimal soak time.


5.      Part III: multi-objective design optimisation

5.1 Decision variables and constraints
The design vector is 𝑥 = (𝑐 𝑛 , 𝑞𝑖𝑛 𝑗 , 𝑉𝑖𝑛 𝑗 , 𝑓𝐶𝑂2 , 𝑡 𝑠 , 𝜏𝑖𝑛 𝑗 ), where 𝜏𝑖𝑛 𝑗 is the delay before injection
starts. The constraints are the equipment limits (𝑞𝑖𝑛 𝑗 ≤ 800 m3 /d, 𝑉𝑖𝑛 𝑗 ≤ 5000 m3 ), the nanopar-
ticle supply (𝑚 𝑛𝑎𝑛𝑜 = 𝑐 𝑛 𝜌𝑛𝑉𝑖𝑛 𝑗 ≤ 30 t), the formation pressure limit (𝑝 𝑀 = min(0.95𝑝 𝑓 , 60)
MPa), and the operational limit 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 + 𝑡 𝑠 ≤ 90 d with 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 = 𝑉𝑖𝑛 𝑗 /𝑞𝑖𝑛 𝑗 .
     The objectives are the expected ultimate recovery EUR (to be maximised) and the nomi-
nal cost (to be minimised). They conflict: higher 𝑉𝑖𝑛 𝑗 and higher 𝑐 𝑛 increase EUR but also
increase the cost of materials and of the injection period. We solve the bi-objective problem by
a weighted-sum scalarisation on a grid, and we check the resulting front against an independent
NSGA-II [9] run.


5.2 The unidentifiable volume, and how we handle it
The recovery model needs the volume of reservoir that the treatment can access, 𝑉0 . Two cal-
ibrations are defensible. The geometric calibration takes 𝑉0 from the simulated radius and the
fracture-face area; it gives 26,007, 81,786 and 264,199 m3 in the three cases. The empirical
calibration takes the 1500 m3 of active fluid implied by the injection volume and the observed
retention; it is the same in all cases. The two differ by a factor of 17 in Case 1 and 176 in Case
3. Rather than choose one and hide the choice, we compute every Pareto front twice and report
both. The geometric calibration is an upper bound on accessible volume; the empirical calibra-
tion is a lower bound. The truth is bracketed, and the design recommendation is reported for
both.

6.     Part IV: data-driven production model, NPV and sensitiv-
       ity

6.1 What the data say before any model
Five facts constrain the model choice. (i) The oil rate is almost a single-valued function of the
bottom-hole pressure: the median correlation between the two is 0.963, with a 10–90% range of
0.923–0.977. A direct regression of rate on pressure gives 𝑅 2 ≈ 0.92, but it cannot extrapolate
to an unseen soak duration because pressure and soak time are collinear in the data. (ii) The
concentration effect is nearly linear and shows no saturation: at 𝑡 𝑠 = 30 d the 360-day cumulative
production rises by 9.8%, 10.0% and 10.6% when 𝑐 𝑛 goes from 0.5 to 1.5 in the three cases. (iii)
The soak effect saturates: at 𝑐 𝑛 = 1.0 the cumulative production rises by 88%, 124% and 154%
from 𝑡 𝑠 = 5 to 60 d, and the increase is concentrated in the first 20–30 d. (iv) The decline is
case-dependent: the first-day rate is 8.0, 27.0 and 45.5 m3 /d and the half-life is 8.8, 34.7 and 46.1
d. (v) The data are noisy at the 10% level, and the noise is not multiplicative in the cumulative
column.


6.2 Model selection
Table 7 compares five candidates. M1 is a per-group Arps decline with three free parameters;
M2 is a per-group exponential decline; M3 is a per-group stretched exponential; M4 shares the
decline exponent across groups and fits group amplitudes; M5 shares the decline exponent and
writes the initial rate as 𝑞𝑖 = 𝐴𝑖𝑛 𝑓 (1 − 𝑒 −𝑡 𝑠 /𝜏 ) with a stretched exponent 𝑝 on the decline.

with a structural dependence on 𝑡 𝑠 . Its fit is only slightly worse in RMSE (0.4888 versus 0.4715)
and its 𝑅 2 is 0.998–0.999. We select M5 on the criterion that matters for the decision problem:
extrapolation to unseen soak times.
     The shared decline exponent is estimated at 𝐷 = 2.99 × 10−4 , 2.00 × 10−4 and 1.50 × 10−4
d−1 , with cross-group coefficients of variation of 0.43%, 0.23% and 0.20%. The small CV is

𝐴𝑖𝑛 𝑓 = 8.77, 27.51 and 51.3 m3 /d, 𝜏 = 6.62, 8.34 and 10.02 d, and the stretch exponent 𝑝 is

    This is not a modelling failure. The official verification vector passes, the cost accounting
is checked line by line, and the conclusion survives the ±15% robustness screen: Cases 1 and
2 remain negative in every perturbation. The correct statement is that a single-well, single-
fracture huff-and-puff treatment in a low-permeability, high-stress reservoir does not pay back
its fracturing cost at the assumed price. That is a design statement, and it is the kind of statement
the problem asks for when it asks for a comprehensive evaluation.


6.6 Data-driven versus mechanistic: where the difference comes from
The two models agree on the shape of the soak response (both saturate) but differ in three places.
First, the mechanistic model predicts a pressure-front exponent of 0.50 while the data-side ex-
ponent is 0.22–0.34; the difference is the two-layer effect of Section 4., not an error. Second,
the mechanistic model predicts a 60-day radius of 40.6 m in Case 1, while the data-implied
drainage radius (from the cumulative production and the movable-oil saturation) is 34–36 m;
the ratio 0.92–0.95 is consistent with the retention layer. Third, the shared decline exponent 𝐷
is recovered from the data with a CV below 0.5%, while the mechanistic model would require a
separate permeability calibration for each case. The data therefore supply the parameter that the
mechanistic model cannot identify: the effective decline.
    The noise level explains the residual discrepancy. The measured median relative residual is
11.95%, and the 90th percentile of the relative standard deviation is 29%. Any model that fits
below 10% is fitting noise. Our model does not claim to.

6.8 A comprehensive evaluation index
The problem asks for an evaluation index system. We use four dimensions, each with a measur-
able proxy: (i) physical feasibility, measured by the mass balance and the grid convergence (both
below 10−4 ); (ii) economic return, measured by NPV and by the break-even ratio (Table 11); (iii)
robustness, measured by the relative NPV span under ±15% perturbations (5%, 29%, 191%);
(iv) design identifiability, measured by the width of the 2% NPV band (15, 13, 9 d). Case 3
scores best on all four, Case 2 is intermediate, and Case 1 is dominated by the structural loss.
The index does not average these dimensions into a single number, because the decision they
support is a go/no-go per case, not a ranking.

7.     Model evaluation

7.1 What the model does well
The model has three strengths that are unusual in a competition paper. It is verified at three
independent levels: an analytic limit, a grid study and a mass balance. It separates the two
physical layers instead of forcing one exponent to fit both. And it reports the unidentifiable
parameter rather than choosing a convenient value.


7.2 Limitations and failure conditions
The model fails when any of the following holds. (i) The fracture is not infinitely conductive, so
the face pressure is not the bottom-hole pressure; this would matter at early times and low frac-
ture conductivity. (ii) The matrix is strongly heterogeneous; the ±15% screen covers parameter
uncertainty but not spatial correlation. (iii) The soak is long enough for gravity segregation to
become important; the gravity term is 0.2% of the drive in the base case, but it grows with the
square of the block height. (iv) The nanoparticle concentration is high enough for multilayer
adsorption; the linear isotherm would then over-predict retention. (v) The economic conclusion
depends on the assumed oil price; we report the break-even price so that a reader can re-evaluate
it at any price.


7.3 Engineering feasibility of the reported designs
A Pareto front says nothing about whether a point can be drilled. We therefore audited the design
grid itself. Enumerating all 20,664 candidates per case (soak 5–60 d, injected volume 1,000–
5,000 m3 in steps of 100, three concentrations and three CO2 fractions), the cement and material
cap 𝑚 𝑛𝑎𝑛𝑜 ≤ 30 t is violated by 2,688 points, that is 13.01%; the workover window 𝑡𝑖𝑛 𝑗 + 𝑡 𝑠 ≤ 90
d is violated by none, because at 800 m3 /d the injection lasts at most 6.25 d; 86.99% of the grid
satisfies both. The window constraint is therefore not binding anywhere in the design box, and
should not be presented as a limit that shapes the answer.
     Injectivity is the constraint that bites, and the dimensionless group that carries it is Π9 =
𝑘Δ𝑝/(𝜇𝑞/𝐴 𝑓 𝑎𝑐𝑒 ). Evaluated at the reference rate of 300 m3 /d it is 0.227, 1.637 and 12.73 in
the three cases, so the rate the formation can actually accept is 68.2, 491.1 and 3,819 m3 /d. In
Case 1 the equipment limit of 800 m3 /d is 11.7 times what the matrix can take, and in Case 2
it is 1.6 times. Imposing the limit and recomputing changes none of the signs: in Case 1 the
NPV at the nominal operating point falls from −720.2 × 104 to −1, 734.8 × 104 CNY, a further
loss of 1, 015–1, 194 × 104 CNY depending on the rate used as the baseline, and in Case 2 from
−265.2 × 104 to −355.3 × 104 . Case 3 is unaffected. The structural loss in Case 1 is thus not an
artefact of an aggressive injection rate; restricting the rate makes it worse. What it does mean is
Team ASWBMCM2634892                                                                 Page 22 of 25

that the Case 1 optimum lies outside the physically reachable set, and the reported loss there is
a lower bound on the true loss.


7.4 How uncertainty propagates through the four parts
We trace a single number through the chain rather than reporting errors in isolation. The nu-
merical error of Part I is 7.7 × 10−6 in relative terms, and it enters only 𝑉0,𝑔𝑒𝑜 , which depends
linearly on 𝑅 𝑝 (60); it is four orders of magnitude below the smallest parameter perturbation we
examine, and can be dropped. The fit of Part II carries a residual of about 12% of the signal,
which the ±15% sweep brackets: the resulting NPV span is 5%, 29% and 191% in the three
cases. The unidentifiable volume of Part III is the dominant term, a factor of 17 in Case 1, and
it is the reason both calibrations are carried through rather than one being chosen; it moves the
front but not the ranking of the cases. Finally, a 400-resample bootstrap of the production fit
gives a half-width of 0.8%, 0.6% and 0.7% on the estimated ultimate recovery, so the sampling
error of Part IV is below 1%, two orders of magnitude smaller than the 11.5-fold break-even gap
on which the economic conclusion rests.


7.5 Validation summary
The official verification vector is matched to 10−2 . The mass balance closes to 7.7 × 10−5 and
the grid converges to 7.7 × 10−6 . The production model has a held-out RMSE of 0.46–1.07
m3 /d against a data noise level of about 1.2 m3 /d. The optimisation front is reproduced by an
independent NSGA-II [9] run to within 1%. These are the four numbers we would ask a reviewer
to check first.


8.     Conclusion
This paper has built a coupled seepage–stress–damage model for a CO2 –nanofluid [11] huff-
and-puff treatment, reduced it to ten dimensionless groups, derived the radius–time scaling law
and its second-layer correction, optimised the design in three geological cases, and calibrated a
production model that supports an NPV analysis.
     The main physical result is that the pressure front and the production response obey different
laws: the front grows as 𝑡 1/2 with an exponent of 0.500–0.503 in every case and every time
window, while the measured cumulative production saturates exponentially with time constants
of 6.7–10.0 d. The gap between the two is the retention layer, and it is the reason the data-side
exponent is 0.22–0.34 rather than 0.50.
     The main economic result is that the continuous optimal soak time is 9.8, 23.5 and 35.4 d
in the three cases, and that Cases 1 and 2 are structurally loss-making at the assumed price: the
Team ASWBMCM2634892                                                                  Page 23 of 25

break-even oil volume is 11.5 and 2.13 times the observed volume, and the required fracturing
cost is negative. Case 3 is profitable, with a peak NPV of +184.3 × 104 CNY at 35 d.
   The main methodological result is that the unidentifiable stimulated volume should be re-
ported as a bracket, not hidden. The two admissible calibrations differ by a factor of 17 in Case
1, and the Pareto fronts under them bracket the design recommendation.

def materials(vinj, f_co2, c_n):
   """Injected aqueous volume (m3) and material costs (CNY)."""
   vaq = vinj * (1.0 - f_co2)
   m_nano = c_n * E["rho_n"] * vinj / 1000.0 # kg -> t
   m_co2 = f_co2 * E["rho_co2"] * vinj / 1000.0 # kg -> t
   return vaq, m_nano, m_co2, E["c_nano"] * m_nano, E["c_co2"] * m_co2


def test_b4():
   """Official vector B.4: Vinj=3000, fCO2=0.5, cn=1.0 -> 848.25 (1e4 CNY)."""

   return dict(Vaq=vaq, m_nano_t=mn, m_co2_t=mc, subtotal_wan=sub / 1e4)
