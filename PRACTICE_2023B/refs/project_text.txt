         Desulfurized Ash in Cotton-Stalk Pyrolysis:
 Compositional Signal, Species Tiers, and a Prediction Envelope

       Background and Task Co-pyrolysing cotton stalk with desulfurized fly ash (DFA) is promoted
as a way to upgrade the gas, yet the two annexes of Problem B report four product yields that must
sum to one and a separate gas table in a different unit, with a declared relative error near 5 percent
on every cell. We ask five questions: whether DFA changes the product distribution and by how
much, how each gas species responds, whether cellooligosaccharide (CE) and lignin (LG) differ at
equal ash loading, which mechanism and kinetics are consistent with those responses, and what can
be predicted at an untried loading. The annexes sample the three mixtures on grids that only partly
coincide, so every comparison is built on the levels they share.
       Modeling Framework Because the four yields are one composition, the analysis works in
isometric log-ratio (ILR) coordinates on a three-part simplex instead of four independent regressions.
Question 1 is answered by an ILR linear model in the ash mass fraction. Question 2 adds a species-
level, noise-calibrated tiering and a mole-fraction bridge between the two annexes. Question 3 turns
the fit into a paired contrast at equal ash level. Question 4 combines a centred log-selectivity, a gas-
phase carbon account, and an isothermal iso-conversion ridge. Question 5 propagates the fitted
residual through a Monte-Carlo prediction envelope and checks it by leave-one-out.
       Results per Question DFA reshapes the distribution rather than diluting it: on a dry-ash-free
basis the ash cannot lower a yield mechanically, yet for cotton stalk tar falls by -37.7 percent between
the zero-ash and the richest level, while the dominant lignin product stays inside its noise floor. The
strongest gas signal is hydrogen in cotton stalk, whose span is 14.47 times its own floor. At equal
ash level CE and LG separate most sharply on char, with a mean gap of -33.27 points, and water is
the only product that changes sign. The gas-phase carbon account falls by 77.2 percent along the
CE series, which links the yield shifts to a carbon-partition change. The cotton-stalk leave-one-out
error is 5.50 percent, the smallest of the three mixtures.
       Validation and Limitation Mass closure holds to machine precision, and every reported num-
ber is recomputed from the stored result files. A perturbation audit of the fitted parameters and the
query points leaves a single headline reversal, 6.67 percent of the feasible model runs. The main
limitation is that neither annex reports the pyrolysis temperature, so the kinetics reduce to an iso-
conversion ridge and no rate law is identified; the CE and LG series also show a lack-of-fit ratio of
3.42 or more, which marks their predictions as trend-only.

Keywords: Compositional data; Isometric log-ratio regression; Noise-calibrated species tiering;
Paired contrast at equal ash level; Selectivity and carbon accounting; Monte-Carlo prediction enve-

                                        I. Introduction

1 . 1 Background
     Cotton stalk is one of the largest dry agricultural residues in the cotton belt, and pyrolysis
converts it into a solid char, a condensable tar, water, and a non-condensable gas whose hydrogen
content determines its value as a fuel or a reducing agent [1, 2]. Ash-rich industrial residues such as
desulfurized fly ash (DFA) are abundant, and their lime and sulfate content makes them plausible
catalysts for tar cracking and carbon dioxide uptake [3, 4]. Co-processing therefore looks attractive,
but it raises a measurement question that a yield table alone can answer only if the table is read
correctly: the four product yields are percentages of one dry-ash-free mass, so they are a composition
with three degrees of freedom, not four independent responses.


1 . 2 Restatement of the Problems
     Write a for the DFA mass charged per 100 g of dry biomass, r = a/100, and w = r/(1 + r) =
a/(100 + a) for the ash mass fraction of the feed. For mixture c ∈ {CS, CE, LG} and product
                                        (c)                                                      (c)
p ∈ {Tar, Water, Char, Syngas}, let yp be the yield in wt.% (daf), and for gas species g let zg be
the yield in mL/g (daf). The five tasks are:
                            (c)
 1. estimate how each yp responds to w, and decide for each product whether the response exceeds
     the declared experimental error;
                            (c)
 2. describe how each zg responds to w, and present the gas composition in a form that is com-
     parable across mixtures;
 3. compare CE with LG at the same ash level, decide which product and gas gaps are real, and
     explain the pattern;
 4. propose a reaction mechanism consistent with Questions 1–3, and determine which kinetic
     parameters the data can and cannot identify;
              (c)
 5. predict yp on a requested ash grid for a mixture with no further experiments, with an uncer-
     tainty statement.


1 . 3 Our Work
     This paper delivers five linked results. (i) An ILR linear model of the full composition, with
a noise-calibrated decision for all twelve product–mixture combinations; tar and water respond in
every mixture, char responds only through a derived column. (ii) A species-level tiering by absolute
span and a mole-fraction bridge that shows the gas annex and the product annex are not the same
measurement. (iii) A paired CE–LG contrast on eight product levels and five gas levels, which sep-
arates the feedstocks on tar, char, hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane but not on water. (iv) A
mechanism reading in three independent signatures, and an honest kinetic result: an iso-conversion
ridge, not a rate law. (v) A prediction table on a seven-level grid with 5–95% intervals, whose
reliability is established by leave-one-out rather than asserted. Every number printed below is re-
computed from data/results_*.json by code/verify_numbers.py; the 547 stored claims and
their provenance are itemised in that script.

                                      II. Problem Analysis
     Three features of the data decide the whole modelling strategy.
     The response is a composition. Each Annex I row sums to 100 by construction (the largest
observed deviation is 1.4 × 10−14 ), so the four yields carry three degrees of freedom. Fitting four
separate regressions would count the same information twice and would produce coefficients that
cannot be compared across mixtures, a defect of ordinary univariate treatment of shares that has been
documented repeatedly [5, 8]. Working in log-ratio coordinates removes both defects and makes the
coefficients interpretable as elasticities: the fitted model predicts shares, and the inverse transform
returns a composition that sums to one exactly [6, 9].
     The dry-ash-free basis removes dilution. Because yp is expressed per gram of dry ash-free
biomass, adding inert ash cannot lower a yield by replacing fuel with ash. Any change in yp is
therefore chemical, or an artefact of the normalisation, and the paper is careful to distinguish the
two. This is why the phrase “the ash dilutes the products” is not available as an explanation here,
and why the observed tar loss in cotton stalk is a statement about chemistry rather than about mass
balance.
     The declared error is the object of inference. Every cell carries a relative error near 5%, so
a smooth curve drawn through four points is not evidence of a trend. Three thresholds are therefore
defined and kept separate: a step threshold for adjacent levels, a difference threshold for the paired
CE–LG contrast, and a trend threshold for a whole series. They answer different questions and are
never substituted for one another. A ratio of span to threshold above one is reported as a signal, and
the denominator is always named, because the same word “significant” would otherwise mean three
different things in three sections.
     Two secondary features matter as well. First, the two annexes use different units (mass fraction
against volume per gram), so a bridge is needed before they can be discussed together; the bridge is a
diagnostic, not a correction. Second, three product columns are stored as formulas rather than mea-
surements (CS syngas, CE char, and LG tar), which makes their fitted responses partly arithmetic;
these cells are flagged wherever they are used.


                         III. Assumptions and Justifications
  1. Independent 5% relative error. Each reported cell is treated as a measurement with inde-
     pendent relative error ε = 0.05; this is the only error statement the problem supplies, and no
     replicate exists to estimate it. Used by every threshold in Sections 5–9 and by the surrogate
     noise simulation.
  2. Exact closure. The four product yields of a row sum to unity, so the composition lives on the
     simplex. Verified on all rows (maximum deviation 1.4 × 10−14 ); used by the ILR transform.
  3. Uniform preparation precision. Columns within one annex were measured with the same
     precision, so a single ε applies to all of them. Structural, not testable from the annex; used
     when pooling residuals.
  4. Smooth, linear response in ILR coordinates. Over the sampled ash range each ILR coordi-
     nate is linear in w. Tested by a lack-of-fit ratio against the surrogate noise floor; it holds for
Team # apmcm2699001                                                                          Page 3 of 27



     CS and is rejected for CE and LG, which are then reported as trend descriptions only.
 5. Comparable pyrolysis conditions across feedstocks. CE and LG were pyrolysed under the
     same conditions as CS, so a CE–LG contrast at equal ash level is a feedstock contrast. Partly
     supported by the shared level grid; used by Section 7 and by the ranking of mechanisms in
     Section 8.
 6. The gas annex measures relative composition only. Annex II volumes are used to compare
     species and mixtures, never to close a mass balance against Annex I. Tested by the bridge ratio,
     whose range shows the two annexes do not report the same quantity.
 7. A reporting threshold of 5 mL/g (daf) separates major from minor species. This is a pre-
     sentation convention, not a physical constant; the interval of thresholds that preserves every
     tier is reported, and the sensitivity of the tiers is measured.
 8. The isothermal endpoint identifies only the product k(T )t. With one temperature and one
     holding time, the pre-exponential factor and the activation energy cannot be separated. Used
     in Section 8: an iso-conversion ridge is reported instead of a point estimate.
 9. Stability is assessed by perturbation, not replication. With no replicate runs, stability is
     measured by perturbing inputs, thresholds, and query points, and by leave-one-out refitting.
     Used in Sections 9 and 10.
10. The prediction grid lies inside the observed support. All seven requested ash levels fall be-
     tween the smallest and largest calibrated levels of each mixture, so the exercise is interpolation;
     the boundary check confirms this and flags the levels that sit on the support edge.

     Symbols are listed with their ranges in Appendix A. In the body, w is the ash mass fraction of

4 . 2 Data Sources and Structure
     Annex I contains 25 product rows (CS 9, CE 8, LG 8) and four product columns, 100 cells in
all; Annex II contains 16 gas rows (CS 6, CE 5, LG 5) and 54 + 25 + 25 = 104 gas cells, with species
counts 9, 5, 5. The two grids are nested rather than identical: Annex I uses nine levels for CS and
eight for CE and LG, Annex II six and five. The shared product levels number eight for the CE–LG
contrast, and the shared gas levels number five. The level 100/100 means a DFA mass fraction of
50.0000%, not pure ash; the mapping w = r/(1 + r) is applied before any fitting, and the unequal
spacing of r (steps of 10 up to 60, then 20) is one reason the response is modelled in w rather than
in r. Both annexes are used exactly as published in the problem statement [16]; no value is edited,
imputed, or rescaled.

4 . 3 Cleaning and Cross-Checks
     No cell is missing. Three product columns are derived by subtraction (CS syngas, CE char, LG
tar; 24 cells), and they are marked in every table and every claim. Closure holds to 1.4 × 10−14 . A
surrogate experiment draws 4000 compositions with independent 5% multiplicative noise, recloses
them, and transforms them by ILR; the standard deviation of the transformed surrogate is the noise
floor used in Section 5. Its value, 0.0502 on the CS grid and 0.0501 on the CE and LG grids, is close
to the analytic expectation for a four-part composition and confirms that the declared error and the
fitted residual are on the same scale.


4 . 4 Rationality of the Comparison
     The declared 5% is a property of the measurement, not an accuracy target for the model; a
model whose residual is smaller than the floor is not “better”, it is merely consistent. Conversely, a
fitted residual above the floor indicates lack of fit, not precision. This asymmetry is used explicitly:
CS has a residual below the floor, so its linear ILR model is accepted; CE and LG have residuals
above it, so their models are demoted to trend descriptions. The three derived columns are treated
as arithmetic consequences of the other three, and a response that appears only in a derived column
is never promoted to a chemical finding.


          V. Question 1: Product Yields against Ash Loading

5 . 1 Mechanism and Rationale
     DFA brings lime, sulfate, and silica into the bed. Lime is a carbon dioxide sorbent and a tar-
cracking contact; sulfate can act as an oxygen carrier; silica is largely inert [3, 4]. Two responses
are therefore plausible on chemical grounds: tar should fall as volatiles are cracked, and gas should
rise as the fragments become non-condensable. Char is expected to be the least responsive product,
because the dry-ash-free basis already removes the inert fraction and because char is the residue that
the ash does not consume. The data are asked whether these expectations survive the noise floor,
not whether they are plausible.

5 . 3 Results
     Table 1 reports the twelve product–mixture combinations, Figure 1 the fitted curves, and Fig-
ure 2 the span-to-floor ratios. Fitting is a six-parameter linear least squares per mixture on 27 (CS)
or 24 (CE, LG) observations; the three-coordinate fit takes a median of 189.7 ms in the reference
implementation, so the cost is negligible and the algorithm is ordinary least squares on a 27 × 2
design.
     Tar falls in cotton stalk (-37.7%, a span of 7.33 against a floor of 1.45) and in lignin (-54.7%,
span 9.87 against 1.13), but rises in CE (31.6%) and in the CE series the rise is 10.86 against a
floor of 4.21. Water moves the other way in CS (+15.6%) and LG (+54.8%) and falls sharply in
CE (-41.1%). Char is flat in CS (ratio 0.259) and in LG (ratio 0.208) but rises by 14.8% in CE; that
rise belongs to a derived column, so the chemical claim is withheld. Syngas rises in CS (+10.2%)
and LG (+26.2%) and falls in CE (-16.5%). The answer to “is DFA significant” is therefore: for tar
and for water, yes, in every mixture; for syngas, yes in two of three; for char, no measured column
moves outside its floor, and the single apparent char response is arithmetic.

          VII. Question 3: CE against LG at Equal Ash Level

7 . 1 Mechanism and Rationale
     Cellooligosaccharide is a carbohydrate model compound and lignin an aromatic polymer, so
their pyrolysis chemistry should differ in a specific direction: the carbohydrate should favour con-
densable anhydro-sugars and hydrogen-rich gas, and the lignin should favour a cross-linked char
and methoxy-derived methane and carbon dioxide [1, 2]. The interesting question is not whether
they differ, but which products carry the difference and whether the ash changes the size of the gap.

7 . 4 Validation and Conclusion
     The contrast is computed at equal w, not at equal r, because w is the physical ash fraction; using
r would compare different solids loadings. The count denominators are stated in every row. Derived
columns are flagged through the char row: CE char is the derived column and its gap, although large,
is the arithmetic mirror of the tar and syngas gaps, so the char separation is reported as consistent
with, not independent of, the tar result. We conclude that CE and LG differ in feedstock chemistry
on tar, char, hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and ethane, and not on water.


                 VIII. Question 4: Mechanism and Kinetics

8 . 1 Mechanism and Rationale
     The yield data cannot identify elementary steps, but they can select among candidate channels.
Three independent signatures are available: the sign and size of the log-selectivity γp , which says
whether a product’s share grows or shrinks with ash; the gas-phase carbon account nC , which says
whether carbon leaves the solid or stays in it; and the shape of the gas response, which separates a
cracking channel (more H2 and CO, less tar) from a carbonation channel (less CO2 , more H2 through
the water–gas shift, water consumed). The two channels are not exclusive, and different feedstocks
can give them different access.

9 . 3 Support and Boundary
                  The support check is the guard against reading a forecast as a measurement. All seven query
levels lie inside the calibrated range of each mixture, so no point is an extrapolation; the check
nevertheless marks five levels as boundary stars and twelve as edge-adjacent, because they sit within
one grid step of the support edge and the linear model has no information beyond it. Widening
the grid would not change the predictive mean much, but it would invalidate the interval, which
is why the request is answered only on the stated grid. A counter-intuitive consequence is that the
uncertainty, not the fit, is what fails first outside the support: the point forecast of LG tar at w = 0.60
would still look smooth while its interval would be meaningless.

                       X. Sensitivity Analysis and Error Analysis

10 . 1 Threshold Paths
      The declared error enters every decision, so it is scanned rather than fixed. Over the open
interval (0.012934, 0.065646) every trend call on the product side is preserved; each endpoint flips
exactly one cell, the lower endpoint making CS char significant and the upper endpoint making CS
ethylene non-significant. The interval is open because the endpoints are the flip points themselves.
The same scan covers 31 cells in total (twelve product series and nineteen gas series), and within
the interval 25 step counts change even though no trend call does: a cell can keep its direction and
lose its resolved steps. That asymmetry is the unfavourable half of the story and it is the reason the
paper reports steps and trends side by side instead of choosing one.
      The tier threshold is scanned the same way. Every assignment in Table 2 is preserved for any
threshold in the open interval (2.50, 5.40); the lower endpoint promotes CE methane to major, the
upper endpoint demotes CS carbon monoxide, and a threshold of 8 mL/g would move three species.
Since 5 mL/g is a reporting convention and not a physical constant, the tier table is a description of
the data at a stated convention, and the interval is the correct way to report it.


10 . 2 Perturbation Audit
      A structured audit perturbs each input and each query point by ±10% and records whether a
headline call changes. Of 42 attempted runs, twelve never reach the model (they are control cases
that test the harness), fourteen only move the query point, and sixteen change a model input; of those
sixteen, fifteen are feasible and one, a raised conversion target, is infeasible as posed because α ≥ 1
makes the endpoint equation undefined. Exactly one feasible perturbation reverses a headline call:
Team # apmcm2699001                                                                                                              Page 22 of 27



a 10% rise in the tier threshold moves CS carbon monoxide from major to minor. That is 6.67% of
the fifteen feasible runs, and it is the same flip that the threshold scan identifies, which is a useful
consistency check between two independent procedures.
      The audit also shows which parameters are load-bearing. The intersection between the eleven
binding identifiers and the twenty-one perturbation sources is not empty: the pyrolysis temperature
and the relative error appear in both, so the model is not insulated from the assumptions that matter
most. That is the honest reading; a disjoint intersection would have suggested a robustness that the
structure does not have.


10 . 3 Numerical Checks and Cost
      Closure is exact by construction and is verified after every transform: the inverse ILR map
returns rows that sum to one with a largest deviation of 2.8 × 10−14 over the whole prediction table.
The surrogate experiment reproduces the analytic noise scale (0.0502 against an expected 0.05), and
the twelve fitted residuals are all positive and of the expected order. Cost is measured over five serial
repetitions: the full pipeline takes a median of 254.609 ms, of which the Q1 fit takes 189.718 ms;
the remaining questions are below a millisecond each except Q5, whose 4000-draw envelope takes
68.1 ms. The implementation is pure Python on top of NumPy [14], with the normal equations solved
by a hand-written elimination that avoids any dependency on a BLAS runtime, and the figures are
drawn with Matplotlib [15]; the timings are therefore conservative for a compiled environment.

                                                     XI. Conclusion
      DFA is not an inert filler in this system. On a dry-ash-free basis, where dilution cannot operate,
it still moves tar by -37.7% in cotton stalk and by -54.7% in lignin, and it moves water by -41.1% in
Team # apmcm2699001                                                                     Page 23 of 27



CE. The strongest single gas response is hydrogen in cotton stalk, 14.47 times its noise floor. The
two model feedstocks separate on tar and char at every shared level (mean gaps 30.78 and -33.27
points) and on hydrogen, carbon dioxide, methane and ethane, but not on water, whose mean gap
of -1.17 is inside the noise. The gas-phase carbon account identifies the CE series as the one where
carbon leaves the solid most strongly (-77.2%), and the lignin series as the one where it accumulates
(+40.1%). The kinetic endpoint is a ridge, not a rate law: Ea between 100 and 250 kJ/mol with
log10 A from 3.751 to 13.545 reproduces the same conversion. Prediction is reliable for cotton stalk
(leave-one-out 5.50%) and trend-only for the other two (18.71% and 17.08% at their worst cells).


                         XII. Strengths and Weaknesses

12 . 1 Strengths
  • The composition is modelled as a composition. Three ILR coordinates replace four regressions,
     the coefficients are comparable across mixtures, and the inverse transform returns a closed
     composition by construction rather than by clipping. The payoff is visible in the agreement
     between the char span (0.208 in LG) and the char selectivity (0.0555), two statistics that share
     no arithmetic.
  • Every claim carries its own denominator and threshold path. The paper distinguishes 31
     scanned cells from 12 trend calls, 15 feasible perturbations from 42 attempts, and 8 product
     pairs from 5 gas pairs; a reader can recompute any of them from the stored JSON with one
     script.
  • The negative results are reported with the same care as the positive ones. The CS char flat call,
     the CE and LG lack of fit, and the water sign flip are not footnotes; they are the reason the
     prediction table is qualified mixture by mixture.


12 . 2 Weaknesses
  • The independent 5% error is an assumption, not an estimate. If errors are correlated within a
     column, the trend tests are too liberal and more cells would be called significant than the data
     support; the direction of that bias is known and stated, but its size is not measurable without
     replicates.
  • The linear ILR model is rejected for CE and LG (F = 3.42 and 3.52). Their quadratic or log
     alternatives fit better, and the intervals of Section 9 are consequently too narrow at the grid
     ends for those two mixtures; the reported leave-one-out errors are the empirical correction, and
     they exceed 5% in seven of eight cells for those mixtures.
  • The kinetics are not identifiable. No temperature programme or holding time is reported, so
     α = 0.95, T = 800 K and t = 1800 s are assumptions, and the ridge spans 9.8 decades in A; a
     second temperature would be needed before any rate law could be claimed.

import numpy as np


def ilr_basis(k=4):
   V = np.zeros((k - 1, k))
   for i in range(k - 1):
      V[i, : i + 1] = 1.0
      V[i, i + 1] = -(i + 1)
      V[i] = V[i] / np.sqrt((V[i] * V[i]).sum())
   return V


V4 = ilr_basis(4)


def ilr(S):
   S = np.atleast_2d(np.asarray(S, dtype=float))
   lg = np.log(S)
   u = lg - lg.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
   return np.einsum("nk,jk->nj", u, V4)


def ilr_inv(v):
   v = np.atleast_2d(np.asarray(v, dtype=float))
   u = np.einsum("nj,jk->nk", v, V4)
   e = np.exp(u)
   return e / e.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)


def thr_single(y, eps):
   y = np.abs(np.asarray(y, dtype=float))
   return 2.0 * eps * y


def thr_trend(y, eps):
   y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
   return 2.0 * eps * np.abs(y).mean()
